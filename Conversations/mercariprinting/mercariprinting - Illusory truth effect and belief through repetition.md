**ASSISTANT:**
 The psychological phenomenon you're describing is called **"illusory truth effect"** (also known as the "truth effect" or "reiteration effect").

This is the tendency for people to believe information is true simply because they've been exposed to it repeatedly. The more you hear something, the more familiar it becomes, and our brains often mistake that familiarity for truth.

There's also **"semantic satiation"** - but that's slightly different. That's when a word or phrase loses meaning through repetition (like when you say a word over and over until it sounds weird).

A related concept is **"belief perseverance"** - once you've started believing something through repetition, you tend to maintain that belief even when presented with contradictory evidence.

The illusory truth effect is particularly relevant to propaganda, advertising, and misinformation campaigns - which connects directly to the deception patterns you're documenting in your book project. It's one of the fundamental mechanisms that makes "the big lie" technique effective: repeat a false claim often enough, and people start accepting it as fact regardless of evidence.

**Factually speaking:**

A **social experiment** in legitimate research requires:
1. A hypothesis
2. Controlled conditions
3. Systematic data collection
4. Analysis of results
5. Drawing conclusions
6. Contributing to a body of knowledge

What YouTube/social media calls "social experiments" are almost universally **pranks** or **staged scenarios for entertainment**. They:
- Have no hypothesis
- Have no controls
- Collect no systematic data
- Draw no conclusions
- Contribute nothing to scientific understanding
- Are repeated endlessly without synthesis

**Your specific point about repetition:** Yes, this is precisely the illusory truth effect in action. By calling pranks "social experiments" repeatedly across millions of videos, creators:
1. Legitimize what is essentially deceptive content
2. Make it sound educational/valuable rather than just entertainment
3. Potentially evade platform rules against prank content
4. Shield themselves from criticism ("it's science!")

**The logical problem you identified:** If these were actual experiments, the millionth iteration of "what happens when I pretend to steal someone's phone" would be worthless. Real experiments build on previous findings—they don't just repeat the same setup for clicks.

So yes: It's semantic manipulation through repetition. They're pranks being relabeled as "social experiments" until people accept the misuse of the term.
