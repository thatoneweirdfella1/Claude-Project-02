Here's a quick rundown of AOL's key moments:

**The roots go back to 1985.** The service traces its history to an online service known as PlayNET, which licensed its software to Quantum Link (Q-Link), going online in November 1985.

**The name "America Online" came in 1989.** Quantum Computer Services was renamed America Online in 1989, and in October 1989, a new version for Mac and Windows was launched as America Online.

**It became a household name in the early-to-mid 1990s.** AOL introduced millions of consumers to the World Wide Web during the 1990s and early 2000s, famously mailing out floppy disks and CDs with free trial hours. By 1995 the service had reached 1 million members, and by 1996 it had grown to 5 million.

**The flat-rate era started in 1996.** In 1996, America Online ditched its original pay-per-hour system in favor of a flat $19.95 monthly fee, effectively beginning the modern internet era.

So in short, AOL first came online in **1985** (as Q-Link/Quantum), officially became *America Online* in **1989**, and reached mainstream popularity through the **early-to-mid 1990s**.

Great question! The answer depends on what you mean by "the internet," since it happened in stages:

**The early internet (1969)** started as ARPANET, a U.S. Defense Department project connecting a handful of universities and research labs — not available to the public at all.

**The World Wide Web (1991)** was invented by Tim Berners-Lee, which is what made the internet actually usable. The Web opened to the public in 1991 and began to enter general use in 1993–94, when websites for everyday use started to become available.

**The key turning point was 1993.** On April 30, 1993, the World Wide Web was released into the public domain, allowing users to create websites filled with graphics, audio, and hyperlinks. That same year, the launch of the Mosaic browser opened up the web to a new audience of non-academics, and people started to discover how easy it was to create their own web pages. The number of websites grew from 130 in 1993 to over 100,000 at the start of 1996.

**Commercial restrictions fully lifted in 1995.** Websites for use by the general public began to emerge in 1993–94, and following the complete removal of commercial restrictions on internet use by 1995, commercialization of the web led to the dot-com boom.

So in short: **1991** is when the web first opened up, **1993** is when it truly became publicly accessible and easy to use, and **1995** is when it fully opened to commercial use. That mid-90s window is really when everyday people started getting online — which is also right when AOL was taking off with its famous CD mailings!

**ASSISTANT:**
 You're likely thinking of the **Scar Experiment** conducted by psychologist **Robert Kleck** and expanded on by **Kleck and Strenta in 1980**.

Here's how it worked:

Participants were given a fake scar on their face using makeup, shown it in a mirror, and then sent into a social interaction. Right before they went in, the researcher pretended to apply "moisturizer" to keep the scar from cracking — but actually removed it entirely without telling the participant.

So the participant **believed** they had a visible scar. They didn't.

**What happened:**

The participants consistently reported that the people they interacted with were staring at the scar, acting uncomfortable, and treating them differently — when in reality there was no scar at all. Nothing on their face had changed.

**What it proved:**

It demonstrated the power of **self-perception on social interpretation** — that what we believe about ourselves physically shapes how we interpret everyone else's behavior toward us. The participants weren't lying. They genuinely experienced the interactions as different.

It's closely tied to concepts like:
- **Self-fulfilling prophecy**
- **Stigma consciousness**
- **Confirmation bias** in social settings

The takeaway is essentially that **our internal narrative about ourselves can distort our reading of reality** more powerfully than reality itself. We find the evidence we expect to find.
