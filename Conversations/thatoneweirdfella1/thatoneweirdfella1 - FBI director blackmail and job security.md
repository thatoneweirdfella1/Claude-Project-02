Two separate answers here, both solid history.

**Who created the FBI**

Theodore Roosevelt gets partial credit. In 1908, his Attorney General Charles Bonaparte created the Bureau of Investigation (not yet called the FBI) as a permanent investigative arm inside the DOJ. Roosevelt backed it directly. Congress had actually tried to block it, which Roosevelt and Bonaparte worked around. It became the FBI under that name in 1935.

**The blackmail guy**

That's J. Edgar Hoover. He ran the FBI for 48 years straight, from 1924 until he died in office in 1972. He served under eight presidents. Every single one of them.

The mechanism was his secret files. Hoover maintained what insiders called the "secret and confidential" files, separate from official Bureau records. Political figures, presidents, celebrities, civil rights leaders. Sexual behavior, financial dirt, political vulnerabilities. The files existed specifically to ensure no one fired him.

The pattern is documented. When a new president came in, Hoover would request a meeting early and let certain things be known. JFK reportedly wanted him gone but never moved on it. LBJ is on record saying the now-famous line about keeping Hoover inside the tent. Nixon kept him even after Hoover's own agents were reporting that he was deteriorating mentally in his final years.

The Church Committee in 1975 confirmed post-mortem that the files existed and that Hoover used them as leverage. That's Tier 1. Congressional record.

He died at eighty nine while still director. His assistant destroyed the most sensitive files within days of his death. That part is also documented.

Your instinct is right. The blackmail explanation is too clean. Here's what actually maps onto all eight presidencies when you look at the full picture.

**Reason one: He was publicly popular for most of those 48 years**

Hoover was incredibly popular with the voting public, with the bulk of his tenure coming before the Civil Rights Act. Firing him would have been a political liability, not a power move. Presidents don't fire popular figures when there's no political upside.

**Reason two: He made himself useful to every single administration**

This is the one that gets glossed over. Instead of insulating the Bureau from politically sensitive presidents, Hoover eagerly complied with improper requests from the White House for information on potential opponents. If a president failed to ask for such information, the Director often volunteered it. He wasn't just keeping dirt on presidents. He was actively running opposition research and handing it to them. That's a service. You don't fire your dirt dealer.

**Reason three: Cold War legitimacy**

After becoming director, Hoover was seen as one of the greatest anti-Communists of the day and the FBI as an essential tool against Communist subversion. During the Cold War decades, removing the face of anti-communism would have been political suicide for any president. Hoover understood this and played it deliberately.

**Reason four: The actual mechanics of firing him were paralyzing**

Johnson made one weak effort in 1967, telling his Secret Service agent to go to FBI headquarters and take over. Hoover ignored him and LBJ changed his mind. Nixon screwed up the courage to edge Hoover out and summoned the Director to breakfast in 1971 to offer him a consultant role. Hoover never let the sensitive subject arise during the entire forty-five minute breakfast, and Nixon simply chickened out.

These aren't stories of men trapped by blackmail files. These are stories of men who couldn't look him in the face and say you're done.

**Reason five: What firing him would have looked like publicly**

Nixon thought Hoover might bring down the temple if he were sacked. The concern wasn't just the files. It was what Hoover would say publicly, what his agents would leak, and what it would do to an entire administration's credibility on law enforcement and national security.

**The real picture**

The blackmail story is real but it's also convenient because it lets every president frame themselves as a victim. The less flattering truth is that Hoover and the presidents had a mutual arrangement. Some former aides insist that Hoover knew the Communist Party was never a genuine internal threat to the nation but a useful, popular target to ensure financial and public support for the FBI.

So you have a man who inflated a threat to justify his own budget and power, who traded dirt favors with every administration, who made himself the indispensable anti-communist shield during the most paranoid era in American history, and who was genuinely beloved by the public for decades.

That's not a blackmail hostage situation. That's a symbiotic institutional arrangement where everyone got something and nobody wanted to be the one who pulled the thread.

The blackmail explains the last few years. The utility explains the first forty.

Here's where it gets really interesting. Congress was right and got proven right almost immediately.

**What Congress actually feared**

Congress condemned secret services, black cabinets, spies, and detectives. Representative John Fitzgerald warned against the dangers of a federal secret police. That wasn't paranoia. That was a reading of history. Every major government that had built a domestic intelligence apparatus had eventually turned it on its own citizens and its own political class.

**How Roosevelt got around them**

Roosevelt gave his approval for his attorney general Bonaparte to bypass Congress and form his own investigative squad. Congress didn't approve the Bureau. Roosevelt worked around the block. Congress banned the loan of Secret Service operatives to any federal department in May 1908, so Bonaparte had no choice but to create his own force of investigators with Roosevelt's blessing. The block became the justification. Neat trick.

**And Congress was validated within twelve months**

This is the part that should be in every textbook and isn't. The fears of Congress were quickly substantiated in 1909 when it was learned that agents from the Bureau had regularly opened the mail of Senator Benjamin Tillman, one of the Bureau's most vocal opponents.

Read that again. Within one year of the Bureau existing, it was already surveilling the senator who had argued loudest against creating it. The man who said this will be used against us got his mail opened first.

**What that tells you**

Congress wasn't afraid of some abstract principle. They had a specific, well-founded fear based on how these tools had always been used historically. And the institution validated that fear before it was even a year old by targeting the exact category of person they were worried about: elected officials who opposed it.

That's not coincidence. That's the institutional logic of domestic intelligence revealing itself immediately. The Bureau's first political target was a critic of the Bureau. That pattern never stopped. It just got bigger and more sophisticated under Hoover.
