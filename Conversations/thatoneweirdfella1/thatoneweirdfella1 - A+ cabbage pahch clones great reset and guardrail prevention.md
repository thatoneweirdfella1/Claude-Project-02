**ASSISTANT:**
 Cloud seeding is literally throwing particles into clouds to make them produce rain or snow.

**How it works:**

You fly a plane into a cloud and spray fine particles (usually silver iodide) into it. Those particles give water vapor something to stick to and condense around. More condensation = rain or snow falls instead of just floating there.

**What it actually does:**

It can increase precipitation by maybe 5-15% under the right conditions. It's not creating rain from nothing; it's coaxing moisture that's already there to fall sooner or in slightly larger amounts.

**Where it's used:**

- China does it heavily (Olympics, drought relief, agricultural regions)
- UAE uses it constantly (desert, needs water)
- US uses it in some western states
- Russia, Thailand, other countries with water needs or weather concerns

**The legit science part:**

It works. Not dramatically, but measurably. You can increase snowpack or rainfall if conditions are already wet enough.

## THE ACTUAL ANSWER: Bradford Hill Criteria + Weight of Evidence Framework

The Bradford Hill criteria are the best available criteria for causal inference. A weight of evidence approach requires quantifying two aspects: first, the probability that each criterion is true, and second, an estimate of the relative weight for the contribution of each criterion to the overall probability.

The Bradford Hill criteria can be applied quantitatively using discriminant analysis to estimate weights for each criterion, which can then be combined into one overall assessment of the probability that an association is causal.

**The Nine Bradford Hill Criteria:**

1. **Strength of association** — How strong is the correlation?
2. **Consistency** — Do multiple independent sources show the same pattern?
3. **Specificity** — Is the effect specific to this cause, or is it broad?
4. **Temporality** — Did the cause precede the effect?
5. **Biological plausibility** — Is there a logical mechanism?
6. **Dose-response** — Does more exposure = stronger effect?
7. **Coherence** — Does it fit with existing knowledge?
8. **Experiment** — Can it be tested/reproduced?
9. **Analogy** — Are there similar causal relationships?

**Why this solves your problem:**

- **No single evidence type is required** — You evaluate across all types
- **Each criterion gets weighted** — Some matter more than others
- **Transparent methodology** — You document which criteria are met and how much they contribute
