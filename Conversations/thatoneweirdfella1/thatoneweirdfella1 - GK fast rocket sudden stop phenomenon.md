Okay, solid thread. Here's what's actually happening.

**GK Persei** (Nova Persei 1901) is a "very fast nova" — classified that way because of how quickly it brightened and faded. It rivaled the brightness of Vega at peak outburst in 1901.

Right after the explosion, observers noticed something bizarre. The optical nebulosity surrounding it appeared to be expanding at superluminal velocities — faster than the speed of light. This was the first time that had ever been observed in astronomy.

When the apparent expansion suddenly seemed to slow or "stop," what you're actually watching is a geometric illusion collapsing. Here's the mechanism:

What observers were seeing wasn't the actual shell at all. The nova's flash is a hollow spherical bubble of light. A sheet of interstellar dust in front of the nova reflects that light forward, and what expands superluminally is the intersection of the light bubble with the dust sheet — not physical matter moving at all. It's called a **light echo**.

The echo appears as a luminous ring expanding at an apparent rate faster than light because of the geometry of forward scattering. The "kinematically accessible ellipsoid" — an elongated ellipsoid with the nova at one focus and Earth at the other — determines what the observer sees at any moment.

The apparent "stopping" happens because as the light bubble expands outward, the geometry of that ellipsoid changes. The intersection angle shifts, the apparent transverse velocity drops, and the ring appears to decelerate or halt — even though nothing physical stopped at all.

Almost fifteen years after the outburst, the *actual* physical nova shell finally became visible — and that shell has been expanding and decelerating ever since due to interaction with the ambient medium, at a far more mundane six hundred to twelve hundred kilometers per second. Proper motion studies of individual knots show they've suffered only modest deceleration over the century since ejection.

So the short version: nothing stopped. You were never watching matter. You were watching a light geometry. When the geometry stopped producing the illusion, the illusion ended.

My bad. Here's what's actually going on with the Go Fast video.

The object appears to be screaming across the ocean surface at insane speed. Then it seems to slow or stop. The official explanation — and this one actually holds up geometrically — is **parallax**.

The appearance of high speed is an optical illusion caused by the parallax effect as the jet flies past the object. The ocean background is far away, and the camera movement makes a stationary or slow-moving object appear to zoom across the water.

AARO determined the object was likely traveling at around thirteen thousand feet altitude — not near the ocean surface as it appears — and was moving with the wind at speeds somewhere between five and ninety-two mph.

So the "sudden stop" is the jet's own forward velocity creating apparent motion in the target. As the jet passes the object and the angle changes, the apparent lateral speed drops toward zero. Nothing stopped. The jet kept going.

The wrinkle that keeps this interesting: the ATFLIR targeting pod had difficulty locking onto the object because the system uses contrast tracking — it looks for a temperature difference between the target and the background. The object was small and cold, offering little contrast against the cold ocean water. That's why the tracking box keeps expanding and contracting.

The skeptic answer is basically: balloon at altitude + parallax + a targeting system struggling with a cold, small object = the Go Fast video. Whether you buy the altitude estimate AARO is claiming is a separate question, because that's doing a lot of heavy lifting in their analysis.

Got it. The GoFast rocket was spin-stabilized through ascent. After reaching a certain altitude, it deployed a yo-yo de-spin mechanism to reduce its spin rate. Flat earthers noticed the spinning motion suddenly slowed and used it to "prove" the rocket hit a barrier.

A yo-yo de-spin mechanism consists of two lengths of cable with weights on the ends, wrapped around the rocket like a double yo-yo. When the weights are released, the rocket's spin flings them outward, transferring angular momentum to the weights and reducing the spin rate.

So what you're seeing visually is the rotation of the rocket dramatically slowing — which from the onboard camera looks like everything suddenly lurches or stops. The rocket itself kept climbing. It reached three hundred eighty-five thousand eight hundred feet — seventy-three miles — and a top speed of three thousand five hundred eighty miles per hour. That's past the Kármán line. No dome stopped it.

The "firmament hit" interpretation is specifically keying off the spin change, not the rocket's actual velocity or altitude. Two very different things happening at the same moment.
