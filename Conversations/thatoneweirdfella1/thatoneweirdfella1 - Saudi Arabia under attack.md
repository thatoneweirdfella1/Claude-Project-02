**A Blood Moon IS a total lunar eclipse**, but not every lunar eclipse is a Blood Moon.

Here's the breakdown:

- **Lunar Eclipse** — This is the broad category. It happens when Earth moves between the Sun and Moon, casting its shadow on the Moon. There are three types: *penumbral* (faint dimming), *partial* (part of the moon darkens), and *total* (the whole moon enters Earth's shadow).

- **Blood Moon** — This specifically refers to a **total** lunar eclipse. During totality, the Moon turns a deep red/orange color — that's why it's called a "blood moon." The red color happens because Earth's atmosphere bends and filters sunlight, and only the red wavelengths reach the Moon's surface. It's essentially every sunrise and sunset on Earth being projected onto the Moon at once.

So in short:
- All Blood Moons are lunar eclipses ✅
- Not all lunar eclipses are Blood Moons ❌ (partial and penumbral eclipses don't produce the red color)

The March 3rd eclipse was a **total** lunar eclipse, which means yes — it was a full Blood Moon, visible in the early morning hours from the Americas before sunrise.

## 🌑 How Often Blood Moons Occur

**Individual Blood Moons** happen roughly **1–3 times per year** on average, but they're not always visible from every part of the world. So from any one location, you might only see one every couple of years.

---

## 🔢 The Numbers

- There are about **230 total lunar eclipses** (Blood Moons) per century — roughly **2–3 per year** globally
- But any *specific location* on Earth only sees about **1 every 2.5 years** on average
- They're not rare in an astronomical sense, but witnessing a clear, vivid one from your backyard is genuinely uncommon

---

## 🔯 What IS Rare — The Tetrad

What makes the current sequence so talked about is the **Tetrad** — four Blood Moons in a row with no partial eclipses in between. That's much rarer:

- A Tetrad only occurs **about 8 times per century**
