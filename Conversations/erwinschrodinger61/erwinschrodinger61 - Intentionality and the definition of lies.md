# Intentionality and the definition of lies

**HUMAN:**
Does a lie have to be intentional for it to be a lie


**ASSISTANT:**
 This is a genuinely interesting philosophical question, and most definitions of lying do require intent as a core ingredient.

The standard philosophical account (associated with thinkers like Kant and more recently Sissela Bok) holds that a lie requires three things: a false statement, knowledge that it's false, and the *intent* to deceive. Under this view, if you sincerely believe something false and say it, you're not lying — you're just mistaken. The intent to deceive is what distinguishes a lie from an honest error.

That said, there are some complications worth considering:

**Reckless assertion** — If someone states something as fact without caring whether it's true or false, many philosophers argue this is morally equivalent to lying, even if there's no specific intent to deceive. Harry Frankfurt famously called this "bullshit" and argued it's actually *worse* than lying in some ways, because the liar at least respects the truth enough to work around it.

**Self-deception** — Someone might convince themselves of something false so they can repeat it "honestly." In that case, there's a kind of indirect intentionality buried in the process.

**Ordinary language** — In everyday speech, people sometimes say things like "you lied to me!" when someone was just wrong, especially if the stakes were high. So colloquially, the word gets stretched beyond its strict philosophical meaning.

The consensus view, though, is that intent is essential. Without it, the better words are *mistake*, *error*, or *misinformation*. The moral weight of lying comes precisely from the deliberate choice to manipulate someone's beliefs — which is why we judge liars more harshly than people who are simply wrong.
