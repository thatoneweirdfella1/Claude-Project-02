**The Historical Legal Cover They Used**

For a long time agencies argued that a Stingray only captured metadata — phone identifiers, signal strength, location data — and not content. Under a legal doctrine called the third party doctrine, information you voluntarily transmit to a third party, in this case a cell tower, carries no reasonable expectation of privacy. They argued connecting to a cell tower is voluntary, therefore intercepting that connection requires no warrant. That argument allowed Stingray use to proceed under a simple pen register order, which has a far lower legal bar than a warrant.

---

**Why That Argument Has Been Collapsing**

In 2018 the Supreme Court ruled in Carpenter v. United States that accessing historical cell site location data does require a warrant. The court recognized that long term location tracking through cell data reveals the privacies of life in a way that the third party doctrine was never designed to cover. That ruling did not specifically address Stingrays but the logic applies directly and lower courts have been extending it.

Several states have passed explicit Stingray warrant requirements independent of federal law. Others are in process. The FBI has its own internal policy requiring warrants for Stingray use in most circumstances, though that policy has exceptions and is self-enforced.

---

**The Gap That Still Exists**

Federal law has not comprehensively addressed it. The FBI policy is internal, not statutory. In jurisdictions without specific state legislation, the legal requirement is still being litigated case by case. Which means in practice, whether a warrant was required depends on where you are, which court is looking at it, and whether anyone challenges it.

Even where warrants are required, there is a structural issue that makes legal challenges difficult.
