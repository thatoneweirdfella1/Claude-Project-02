**The "Forbidden Fruit" / Reactance Effect**
Psychological reactance theory suggests that when people — especially adolescents — feel their freedom is being restricted or are warned away from something, it can actually increase curiosity and desire for it. **When awareness *does* work**
The research isn't uniformly negative. Campaigns tend to be more effective when they:
- Focus on social norms ("most people don't do this") rather than danger
- Are delivered by peers rather than authority figures
- Avoid sensationalizing the topic
- Give people concrete alternative behaviors

## 1. The Antarctic Treaty (1959)

**What it did:** Demilitarized Antarctica, banned nuclear weapons and testing there, froze all territorial claims, and dedicated the continent to peaceful scientific research.

**Signed:** December 1, 1959 — **entered into force:** June 23, 1961

**Original 12 signatories:** Argentina, Australia, Belgium, Chile, France, Japan, New Zealand, Norway, South Africa, the Soviet Union, the United Kingdom, and the United States — all countries active in Antarctica during the International Geophysical Year of 1957–58.

As of May 2025, 58 countries have signed up as parties to the treaty.

**29 Consultative Parties (voting members):** Argentina, Australia, Belgium, Brazil, Bulgaria, Chile, China, Czech Republic, Ecuador, Finland, France, Germany, India, Italy, Japan, Republic of Korea, Netherlands, New Zealand, Norway, Peru, Poland, Russia, South Africa, Spain, Sweden, Ukraine, United Kingdom, Uruguay, and the United States.

**28 Non-Consultative Parties (non-voting observers):** Austria, Belarus, Canada, Colombia, Cuba, Denmark, Estonia, Greece, Guatemala, Hungary, Iceland, Kazakhstan, North Korea, Malaysia, Monaco, Mongolia, Pakistan, Papua New Guinea, Portugal, Romania, Slovakia, Slovenia, Switzerland, Turkey, and Venezuela.

**Countries NOT part of the treaty** include major nations like China (wait — China IS a member), Iran, Iraq, Saudi Arabia, and most of Africa and Southeast Asia. Iran notably stated in February 2024 that it has property rights in Antarctica and plans to conduct activities there, but it is not a party to the treaty.

## 2. The Outer Space Treaty (1967)

**What it did:** Banned nuclear weapons and WMDs in space, prohibited military bases on the Moon or other celestial bodies, declared space free for all nations to explore, and prevented any country from claiming sovereignty over space or celestial bodies.

**Signed:** January 27, 1967 — **entered into force:** October 10, 1967

**Initiated by:** The United States, the United Kingdom, and the Soviet Union, who opened it for signature simultaneously in all three capitals.

As of October 2025, 118 countries are full parties to the treaty, with another 20 who have signed but not yet completed ratification. This includes essentially all major spacefaring nations.

**Notable non-members** (neither signed nor ratified): A handful of smaller nations, though the list is shrinking. Most significantly, no major spacefaring nation is outside it.

---

## 3. SALT (Strategic Arms Limitation Talks) — I and II

**This one is fundamentally different from the other two** — SALT was not a multilateral treaty open to all countries. SALT was exclusively bilateral — two rounds of conferences and treaties involving only the United States and the Soviet Union.

- **SALT I** — signed May 26, 1972 in Moscow by President Nixon and General Secretary Brezhnev. It froze the number of ICBMs and submarine-launched missiles and created the Anti-Ballistic Missile (ABM) Treaty.

- **SALT II** — signed June 17, 1979 in Vienna by Carter and Brezhnev, but was never ratified by the U.S. Senate after the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan. Both Washington and Moscow nonetheless pledged to adhere to its terms.

So every other country in the world was essentially "not part of" SALT — it was purely a US-USSR affair. It later evolved into the START treaties in the 1990s.
