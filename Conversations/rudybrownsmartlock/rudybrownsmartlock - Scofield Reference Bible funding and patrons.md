In 1901, Scofield became a member of the Lotos Club, an exclusive New York men's club. Joseph M. The four documented backers are Plymouth Brethren Christian businessmen (Ball, Pirie, Fitch, Buss) who shared Scofield's theological framework through the Darbyite tradition. The Untermyer/Lotos Club connection is a legitimate historical question raised by Canfield and others, but it rests on circumstantial inference rather than financial documentation. **How OUP came to publish it**

The connection to Oxford University Press ran directly through Henry Frowde, who was appointed Publisher to OUP in 1880 and had built it into a major Bible-printing operation. Frowde fellowshipped all his adult life with those known as "Brethren" and had distinguished himself as an authority in the printing and binding of Bibles. He achieved at Oxford what was once considered impossible: the distribution of 1,000,000 New Testaments in one 12-hour period. Frowde was not a neutral commercial publisher; he was himself a lifelong member of the Exclusive Brethren, the stricter faction that split from the Plymouth Brethren in 1848 following Darby. He shared Scofield's entire theological framework.

While in England, Scofield visited his friend Robert Scott of Morgan and Scott, publishers of religious books. When Scott learned of Scofield's project, he introduced Scofield to Henry Frowde, head of Oxford University Press. Frowde was very interested and positive about publishing the Reference Bible.

Scofield spent the years 1905 to 1908 largely in Europe where he met Frowde, who was also at work developing a new North American branch for the press. Scofield and Frowde came to an agreement in April of 1909 that landed the Scofield Reference Bible in the hands of readers on both sides of the Atlantic.

So the OUP connection is explained not by institutional prestige seeking out a theological project, but by the fact that the man running OUP's Bible division was a coreligionist who had been waiting for exactly this kind of work. Oxford's imprint then gave Scofield's commentary a credibility it could not otherwise have claimed. His notes on Palestine, on Israel's restoration, on the cursing of nations that failed to support the Jewish people, now carried Oxford's name.

**How it achieved rapid seminary and institutional penetration**

The distribution infrastructure was already in place before the book was printed, because dispensationalism had spent three decades building its own institutional network. Even before the publication of the Scofield Reference Bible, in the last quarter of the nineteenth century, dispensational beliefs were being disseminated in the United States through the Bible Conference Movement and numerous Bible institutes founded across America to promote the new teaching. Among those that arose in the late 1800s were the Nyack Bible Institute, the Boston Missionary Training School, and the Moody Bible Institute.

Scofield himself was embedded at the top of that network. Dwight L. Moody was sympathetic to the broad outlines of dispensationalism and had as his closest lieutenants dispensationalist leaders including Reuben Torrey, James Gray, Cyrus Scofield, and others. These men were activist evangelists who promoted a host of Bible conferences and gave the dispensationalist movement institutional permanence by assuming leadership of the new independent Bible institutes such as the Moody Bible Institute (1886), the Bible Institute of Los Angeles (1907), and the Philadelphia College of the Bible (1914).

Scofield had also already built his own pre-existing audience through a correspondence course. In 1890, Scofield began a Bible Correspondence Course which was eventually taken over by Moody Bible Institute and spread dispensational teaching worldwide.

More important to Scofield was establishing that he was indebted to, as his preface made clear, "the valuable suggestions and co-operation" of such an esteemed group of Moody Movement leaders whose names essentially vouched for the high quality of the final product. In other words, the list of editorial endorsers was deliberately constructed to function as institutional credentialing.

The three things that popularized dispensationalism were the Bible Institute movement, prophecy conferences held in Winona Lake, Philadelphia, Dallas and elsewhere, and then the Scofield Reference Bible itself. In less than a decade it had sold a million copies.

Dallas Theological Seminary became the academic anchor for the next generation. Weber notes that the center of "institutional dispensationalism" was Dallas Theological Seminary, which quickly became the academic and theological "Vatican" of the movement. Its founder and first president was Lewis Sperry Chafer, whose voluminous systematic theology would outline and further define dispensational thought.

**The structural reason it worked**

The Scofield Reference Bible embedded dispensational interpretations directly into the margins of Scripture, making Scofield's notes inseparable from the biblical text itself for many American evangelicals. Its notes taught readers how to interpret Revelation before they'd even finished reading it. That physical format, commentary printed on the same page as scripture rather than in a separate volume, was the innovation that made it sticky. A reader couldn't engage with the text without simultaneously engaging with Scofield's gloss. Once that format was adopted as a standard teaching Bible across the Bible institute network, each graduating class of ministers carried it into their own congregations and institutions.

This is a rich topic that requires separating what Quigley actually wrote from how his work has been received and selectively used afterward. Here's a careful account organized by book and theme.

**Who Quigley Was and Why His Account Matters**

Quigley earned his PhD from Harvard, taught at Princeton, Harvard, and then for 35 years at Georgetown's School of Foreign Service. He was a consultant to the U.S. Department of Defense, the U.S. Navy, and the House Select Committee on Astronautics and Space Exploration. He was not a fringe observer.

Quigley's own statement of his access and his relationship to the network he documented is the essential starting point. In his own words from Tragedy and Hope: "There does exist, and has existed for a generation, an international Anglophile network which operates, to some extent, in the way the radical Right believes the Communists act. In fact, this network, which we may identify as the Round Table Group, has no aversion to cooperating with the Communists, or any other groups, and frequently does so. I know of the operations of this network because I have studied it for twenty years and was permitted for two years, in the early 1960s, to examine its papers and secret records. I have no aversion to it or to most of its aims and have, for much of my life, been close to it and to many of its instruments."

His stated objection was narrow but pointed: his chief difference of opinion was that the network wishes to remain unknown, and he believed its role in history is significant enough to be known.

**The Anglo-American Establishment: The Secret Society and Its Origins**

This book, written before Tragedy and Hope but published posthumously in 1981, is the more focused and explicit of the two. Quigley opens with the founding event:

One February afternoon in 1891, three men were engaged in earnest conversation in London. For these men were organizing a secret society that was, for more than fifty years, to be one of the most important forces in the formulation and execution of British imperial and foreign policy. The three men were Cecil Rhodes, William T. Stead, and Reginald Baliol Brett (Lord Esher). They drew up a plan of organization providing for an inner circle to be known as "The Society of the Elect," and an outer circle to be known as "The Association of Helpers." Within The Society of the Elect, the real power was to be exercised by the leader and a "Junta of Three."

What is not so widely known is that Rhodes in five previous wills left his fortune to form a secret society devoted to the preservation and expansion of the British Empire. And what does not seem to be known to anyone is that this secret society was created by Rhodes and his principal trustee, Lord Milner, and continues to exist to this day. It does not have secret robes, secret handclasps, or secret passwords. It does not need any of these, since its members know each other intimately. It probably has no oaths of secrecy nor any formal procedure of initiation. It does, however, exist and holds secret meetings, over which the senior member present presides. At various times since 1891, these meetings have been presided over by Rhodes, Lord Milner, Lord Selborne, Sir Patrick Duncan, Field Marshal Jan Smuts, Lord Lothian, and Lord Brand.

**The Stated Aims: Federation of the English-Speaking World**

Quigley is explicit about what Rhodes and Milner wanted. In one of his most precise summary passages from The Anglo-American Establishment: "The goals which Rhodes and Milner sought and the methods by which they hoped to achieve them were so similar by 1902 that the two are almost indistinguishable. Both sought to unite the world, and above all the English-speaking world, in a federal structure around Britain. Both felt that this goal could best be achieved by a secret band of men united to one another by devotion to the common cause and by personal loyalty to one another. Both felt that this band should pursue its goal by secret political and economic influence behind the scenes and by the control of journalistic, educational, and propaganda agencies."

The Rhodes Scholarships were explicitly one instrument of this: they "were merely a facade to conceal the secret society, or, more accurately, they were to be one of the instruments by which members of the secret society could carry out his purpose."

Quigley concluded that the secret cabal advanced its power base through a triple-front penetration in politics, the press, and education.

**Funding and Institutional Structure**

Money for the activities of this organization came originally from the associates and followers of Cecil Rhodes, chiefly from the Rhodes Trust itself, and from wealthy associates such as the Beit brothers and Sir Abe Bailey. Since 1925 there have been substantial contributions from wealthy individuals and from foundations and firms associated with the international banking fraternity, especially the Carnegie United Kingdom Trust, and organizations associated with J.P. Morgan, the Rockefeller and Whitney families, and the associates of Lazard Brothers and of Morgan, Grenfell, and Company. The chief backbone of this organization grew up along the already existing financial cooperation running from the Morgan Bank in New York to a group of international financiers in London led by Lazard Brothers.

**The Institutional Network: Round Table to CFR to RIIA**

The organization functioned through certain loosely affiliated front groups, including the Royal Institute of International Affairs (Chatham House), the Institute of Pacific Relations, and the Council on Foreign Relations. In addition, other secret societies are briefly discussed in Tragedy and Hope, including a consortium of the leaders of the central banks of several countries who formed the Bank for International Settlements.

The existence of this Wall Street Anglo-American axis is, Quigley notes, quite obvious once pointed out. It is reflected in the fact that such Wall Street luminaries as John W. Davis, Lewis Douglas, Jock Whitney, and Douglas Dillon were appointed to be American ambassadors in London.

**The Financial Aim: World System of Private Financial Control**

This is the most-cited passage from Tragedy and Hope, and it is Quigley's own framing, not an inference: "The powers of financial capitalism had another far-reaching aim, nothing less than to create a world system of financial control in private hands able to dominate the political system of each country and the economy of the world as a whole. This system was to be controlled in a feudalistic fashion by the central banks of the world acting in concert, by secret agreements arrived at in frequent meetings and conferences. The apex of the systems was to be the Bank for International Settlements in Basel, Switzerland, a private bank owned and controlled by the world's central banks which were themselves private corporations. Each central bank sought to dominate its government by its ability to control Treasury loans, to manipulate foreign exchanges, to influence the level of economic activity."

**The Two-Party System Passage**

Quigley's most widely circulated observation concerns how this network maintained political control across ostensibly competing parties: "The argument that the two parties should represent opposed ideals and policies is a foolish idea. Instead, the two parties should be almost identical, so that the American people can throw the rascals out at any election without leading to any profound or extensive shifts in policy."

He elaborated that the network funded both sides: "Indeed, some of them intended to contribute to both and to allow an alternation of the two parties in public office in order to conceal their own influence and to allow the electorate to believe that they were exercising their own free choice."

**Quigley's Own Sympathy and His Distinction**

A critical and easily misread element: Quigley did not write as a critic of the network's goals. He stated that the intentions and objectives of the group were "largely commendable." His problem was with secrecy and with certain specific policies, not with the broad aim of Anglo-American global coordination. This puts him in a genuinely unusual position: an insider-adjacent historian who documented elite coordination not to expose and condemn it, but because he thought history required it and that the network had become too influential to remain unacknowledged.

**What Happened to the Book**

After Tragedy and Hope was published, something unusual happened. The original edition published by Macmillan sold about 8,800 copies and sales were picking up in 1968 when, as Quigley wrote, they "ran out of stock." When Quigley pursued the matter with a lawyer in 1974, Macmillan told him they had destroyed the plates in 1968. He wrote: "They lied to me for six years, telling me that they would reprint when they got 2,000 orders, which could never happen because they told anyone who asked that it was out of print and would not be reprinted." Copies became collector's items.

For writing certain sections, Quigley was ostracized by the academic community and found his lecturing contracts drying up. When the journalist Robert Eringer went to see Quigley just before his death, Quigley warned him off the subject of Tragedy and Hope, saying it would get Eringer into trouble. After Quigley's death there was a report that his papers had been stolen.

**What Independent Scholarship Has Verified**

A systematic fact-checking study found a consistent pattern: Quigley accurately documented the existence of elite networks including the Round Table Movement, banking influence on World War I, and central bank coordination, but embedded these verified facts within interpretive frameworks. Approximately 60% of Quigley's claims about institutional relationships prove historically accurate, while 40%, particularly assertions of coordinated global domination, secret agreements, and unified conspiratorial intent, lack independent corroborating documentation.

The honest read is that Quigley documented something real, named it plainly, described its aims in his own words, expressed personal sympathy for most of those aims, and objected primarily to secrecy rather than substance. The network's own stated goals, in his account, were Anglophone imperial federation, private financial control over political systems worldwide, and the management of public opinion through control of press and educational institutions. He did not consider these goals sinister. That gap between what he documented and how he evaluated it is itself one of the more interesting features of both books.

This is one of the more documented and verifiable episodes in the history of congressional investigations into elite institutional coordination. Here is a thorough account organized by phase.

**Background: The Cox Committee Clears the Foundations (1952)**

In the fall of 1952, all foundations with assets of $10 million or more received a questionnaire covering virtually every aspect of their operations. The foundations cooperated willingly. In the committee's final report, submitted to Congress in January 1953, the Cox Committee endorsed the loyalty of the foundations, stating: "So far as we can ascertain, there is little basis for the belief expressed in some quarters that foundation funds are being diverted from their intended use." Unhappy with these conclusions, Rep. Reece pushed for a continuation of the work.

**The Reece Committee: Structure and Mandate (1953-1954)**

In April 1954, the House authorized the Reece Committee. Unlike its predecessor, which limited its attention to generalities, the Reece Committee mounted a comprehensive inquiry into both the motives for establishing foundations and their influence on public life. The investigative inquiry was headed by Norman Dodd, a former banker.

The majority of Republicans supported creating the committee, while less than half of Democrats did. Among the 49 GOP congressmen who voted against it were future president Gerald Ford, future senators Hugh Scott and Jacob Javits, and Peter Frelinghuysen, a member of the Council on Foreign Relations.

**Norman Dodd and the Carnegie Minutes**

The most explosive finding came from the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace's own archived records. Dodd sent staffer Katherine Casey to Carnegie headquarters in New York to examine decades of trustee meeting minutes that had been stored away in a warehouse. Joseph Johnson, Carnegie's president and a close friend of former Carnegie president and convicted Soviet spy Alger Hiss, evidently had no idea what was in them.

What Casey found in those minutes was a sequence of deliberations beginning before World War I. The Carnegie Endowment trustees had asked themselves: "How do we involve the United States in a war?" And they answered: "We must control the diplomatic machinery of the United States by first gaining control of the State Department." According to Dodd's Reece Committee report, the minutes documented that the Carnegie Endowment "had already become a powerful policy-making force inside the State Department."

After the war, the trustees reasoned that if they could control education in the United States, they could prevent a return to pre-war life. They then divided responsibilities with other foundations: "They divided the task in parts, giving to the Rockefeller Foundation the responsibility of altering education as it pertains to domestic subjects, but Carnegie retained the task of altering our education in foreign affairs and about international relations."

Dodd stated in his findings that many foundations were intentionally campaigning to involve the United States in wars such as World War I, and that they sought to warp American teaching of history through explicit control of the education system.

**The Ford Foundation's Directive**

Dodd's account of his meeting with Rowan Gaither, president of the Ford Foundation, became another central piece of testimony. As Gaither explained to Dodd: "All of us here have at one time or another served in the OSS, or the European Economic Administration, operating under directives from the White House. We still operate under those same directives. The substance of the directives under which we operate is that we should use our grant-making power to so alter life in the United States that we can be comfortably merged with the Soviet Union."

**What the Formal Report Concluded**

The Reece Committee's formal report conceded that, with several exceptions such as the Institute of Pacific Relations, foundations had not directly supported organizations that in turn operated to support communism. However, the report did conclude that some of the larger foundations had directly supported "subversion" in the true meaning of that term, namely the process of undermining some of the country's vitally protective concepts and principles.

The committee declared that the CFR was "in essence an agency of the United States Government" and that its productions were "not objective but directed overwhelmingly at promoting a globalist concept." The committee found that in the international field, foundations and an interlock among some of them and certain intermediary organizations had "exercised a strong effect upon our foreign policy and upon public education in things international."

Although the promotion of internationalism and moral relativism by foundations concerned the committee, it saw their concentrated power as the more central threat. Even if benign, this power posed a threat to democratic government.

**How the Investigation Was Derailed**

The mechanics of the obstruction are extensively documented by the committee's own general counsel.

According to general counsel René Wormser's 1958 account in Foundations: Their Power and Influence, Hays "was frank enough to tell us that he had been put on the committee by Mr. Sam Rayburn, the Democratic Leader in the House, as the equivalent of a watchdog. Just what he was to 'watch' was not made clear until it became apparent that Mr. Hays was making it his business to frustrate the investigation to the greatest extent possible."

Hays repeatedly interrupted witnesses and committee proceedings with rude, insulting, and inane questions. He interrupted witness Aaron Sargent 246 times during Sargent's 185 minutes of testimony. He crumpled up Dodd's copy of a report and tossed it at him, so that Dodd was forced to read to the packed hearing from a dirty, crumpled document.

This was the only period until 2005 when the Republican Party controlled both houses of Congress and the presidency. Yet Hays was able to persuade other committee members to pull the plug.

The committee's two Democrats, Hays and Gracie Pfost, refused to sign the final report. Their minority report charged that the foundations had "been indicted and convicted under procedures which can only be characterized as barbaric," and accused the chairman and staff of a "deep-seated antagonism toward foundations" that "might well be characterized as pathological."

**Eisenhower's Role**

When direct obstruction by Hays failed to stop the hearings, Major General Wilton B. Persons was sent by President Eisenhower to urge Hays to sabotage the hearings, which Hays did not hesitate to do. Reece and Dodd also received death threats throughout the investigation.

**Why the Report Received No Attention**

The Reece Committee's report, submitted in the midst of the ultimately successful efforts to censure Senator Joseph McCarthy, failed to attract much attention. McCarthy's fall led to a discrediting of all efforts that smacked of redbaiting.

Wormser later wrote that the Reece investigation was "virtually hamstrung from the start to its early demise, which was aided and abetted by the leading newspapers of the country."

**The Permanent Record**

Two primary documents from participants remain. Dodd's testimony was recorded in a long interview conducted by G. Edward Griffin shortly before Dodd's death in 1987. Wormser's 1958 book Foundations: Their Power and Influence, written as counsel to the committee, remains the most complete participant account. Wormser wrote: "It is difficult for the public to understand that some of the great foundations which have done so much for us in some fields have acted tragically against the public interest in others, but the facts are there for the unprejudiced to recognize."

The Dodd report itself is archived and available through the Internet Archive. The full hearing transcripts are also accessible, though extremely lengthy.

One important caveat: Dodd's testimony about the Carnegie minutes and the Gaither statement exists primarily through Dodd's own later recollections, including the Griffin interview made decades after the events. The Carnegie minutes themselves were transcribed onto dictaphone belts by Casey, and Dodd acknowledged that only three people ever heard those recordings, and no formal typed transcript was made. The dictaphone belts were never independently verified by outside researchers, and their current location, if they still exist, is unknown. The formal Reece Committee report and Wormser's book are the more documentarily solid portions of the record.
