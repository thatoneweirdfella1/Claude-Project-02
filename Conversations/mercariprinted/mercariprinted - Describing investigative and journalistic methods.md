**What journalists specifically do has a name too — it's called:**

**"Accountability Journalism"** or **"Watchdog Reporting"**

The actual process they follow looks like this:

1. Form a hypothesis ("I think X is happening")
2. Gather **primary sources** — documents, records, interviews, data
3. **Corroborate** — verify with multiple independent sources
4. Challenge your own findings (look for evidence that disproves you)
5. Only conclude what the evidence actually supports

That last step — actively trying to disprove yourself — is literally what separates real investigation from conspiracy theorizing. Conspiracy theorists seek confirmation. Investigators seek **falsification** (trying to break their own theory).

This is actually a perfect angle for your "Conspiracy Facts" book, because the thing that makes a conspiracy *theory* vs a conspiracy *fact* is whether someone followed that process or skipped it.

**Word breakdown:**

- **Falsification** = testing if something can be proven *wrong* — the gold standard of real scientific thinking

Yes — and there are actually several, each with a strong track record. Here they are, ranked roughly by how widely recognized and proven they are:

---

**1. Forensic Analysis**
Used in law, criminal investigation, and increasingly by civilian researchers. Think of it as the scientific method but applied specifically to *physical evidence* — documents, digital data, audio, images. The name for this when civilians do it online is **OSINT** (Open Source Intelligence). Groups like Bellingcat use this to verify or debunk events using only publicly available information. It's hugely respected.

**2. Legal / Adversarial Method**
This is the courtroom approach. You don't just find truth — you stress-test it by having two opposing sides attack it from every angle. The idea is: if a claim survives someone actively trying to destroy it with counter-evidence, it's probably solid. This is called **adversarial epistemology** — truth through opposition. Think of it like this: the scientific method finds the answer, but the legal method tries to *break* the answer to see if it holds.

**3. Historical Method**
Historians have a strict, documented process — cross-referencing primary sources, checking dates, verifying authorship, looking for bias in who wrote something and *why*. It's less about experiments and more about **triangulation** — meaning, if three unconnected sources from three different contexts all say the same thing, it's probably true.

**4. Peer Review**
This isn't a method on its own, but it's a *verification layer* that sits on top of any of the above. It means other qualified people who have nothing to gain try to find holes in your work. It's what separates "I found something" from "this has been confirmed."

**5. Intelligence Analysis (HUMINT/SIGINT frameworks)**
The CIA, NSA, and other agencies developed their own standardized truth-finding frameworks. One well-known public version is called **ACH — Analysis of Competing Hypotheses**. You list every possible explanation, then eliminate them by evidence rather than picking your favorite and defending it. It's specifically designed to fight confirmation bias.

---

The thing all five share with journalism and the scientific method is this:

> *"It's not about finding what you want to find. It's about building a case so strong that even your enemies can't knock it down."*

For your book specifically, **ACH and OSINT** are probably the two worth adding to your toolkit — because they're designed precisely for the kind of work you're doing: starting with a claim and methodically testing whether it's real or manufactured.

---

**Word breakdown:**
- **Epistemology** = the study of how we know what we know — basically, the philosophy of finding truth
- **Triangulation** = confirming something by finding it from multiple completely unconnected sources
- **ACH (Analysis of Competing Hypotheses)** = an intelligence method where you test all possible explanations against the evidence instead of just defending one theory
- **OSINT** = Open Source Intelligence — investigating using only publicly available information (no hacking, no inside sources required)
- **Confirmation bias** = the human tendency to only notice evidence that supports what you already believe, while ignoring what contradicts it

Here's how, and where it works vs. where it breaks down:

---

**How it works in practice:**

1. You submit your findings, evidence, sources, and reasoning to an AI.
2. You instruct it to argue *against* your conclusion — actively look for holes, logical fallacies, unsupported claims, missing evidence, and alternative explanations.
3. You run it through *multiple* AI systems, not just one. Different models have different training data and different blind spots. Disagreement between them is meaningful.
4. You treat the AI's challenges the same way you'd treat a human peer reviewer — respond to each objection with evidence or revise your claim.

---

**Where it genuinely works:**

- Catching logical inconsistencies
- Identifying unsupported leaps in reasoning
- Flagging when a source is being misrepresented
- Checking if conclusions actually follow from the evidence given
- Finding gaps — what evidence *should* exist if the claim is true but doesn't appear in your work

---

**Where it has hard limits:**

- AI cannot independently verify physical evidence
- AI cannot contact sources or request documents
- AI's knowledge has a cutoff date — anything recent it may not know
- AI can hallucinate false counter-arguments that sound valid but aren't
- AI has no professional reputation on the line, which is part of what makes human peer review trustworthy — humans risk being wrong publicly

---

**Bottom line:** AI peer review is a legitimate *first pass* and a strong *logic checker*, but it cannot fully replace human peer review because it cannot independently investigate — it can only analyze what you give it.

The accurate term for what AI does in this role is **"automated critical analysis"** — not true peer review, but functionally close for internal verification purposes.

---

**Word breakdown:**
- **Logical fallacy** = a flaw in reasoning that makes an argument invalid even if the facts seem correct
- **Automated critical analysis** = using software or AI to systematically find weaknesses in an argument or document
