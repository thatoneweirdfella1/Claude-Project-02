Good question and it's one that cuts through a common misconception. Here's the breakdown:

**The word "Semitic" refers to a language/ethnic group, not a religion.**

Semitic peoples historically includes Arabs, Jews, Assyrians, Babylonians, Arameans, Phoenicians, Canaanites, and others across the Middle East and Horn of Africa. Today the term is largely used in linguistics to refer to Semitic-speaking peoples.

So when you look at modern Semitic-speaking peoples by religion, the rough breakdown is something like this:

**Arabs alone** are the largest Semitic-speaking group today, numbering around 400+ million, and the overwhelming majority are Muslim. Add in non-Arab Semitic speakers (Amharic/Tigrinya speakers in Ethiopia and Eritrea, Maltese, Aramaic-speaking communities, etc.) and you're looking at well over a billion people total who fall under the Semitic linguistic umbrella.

Rough religious breakdown of modern Semitic-speaking peoples:

**~85-90% Muslim** (Arabs primarily, plus others)
**~1-2% Jewish** (around 15-16 million globally)
**~8-10% Christian** (Arab Christians, Coptic-adjacent communities, Ethiopian Semitic speakers, Assyrian Christians, Maltese, etc.)

The practical takeaway: **the vast majority of Semitic-descended people are Muslim, not Jewish.** Which is why the common political use of "antisemitic" as a synonym for "anti-Jewish" is technically imprecise. An act targeting Arabs is also, by definition, antisemitic in the original sense of the word.

That framing gets weaponized in both directions, so worth knowing the actual etymology.
