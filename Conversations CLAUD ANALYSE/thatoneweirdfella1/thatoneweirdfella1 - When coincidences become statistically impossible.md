**ASSISTANT:**
 This is a classic probability question. The answer depends on how improbable each individual "coincidence" is, but here's the core math:

**If each coincidence has a 1-in-10 chance of being random:**

| Coincidences | Probability it's all random |
|---|---|
| 1 | 1 in 10 |
| 2 | 1 in 100 |
| 3 | 1 in 1,000 |
| 4 | 1 in 10,000 |
| 5 | 1 in 100,000 |
| **6** | **1 in 1,000,000** |

**6 independent 1-in-10 coincidences = 1-in-a-million chance it's all random.**

---

**The general formula:** if each coincidence has probability *p* of being random, you need *n* of them where:

> p^n ≤ 0.000001

So it scales fast depending on how "unlikely" each one is:

| Individual odds | # needed for 1-in-million |
|---|---|
| 1 in 2 (coin flip) | 20 |
| 1 in 5 | 9 |
| 1 in 10 | 6 |
| 1 in 100 | 3 |
| 1 in 1,000 | 2 |

---

**The catch:** this only works if the events are truly *independent*. If they share a common cause (which is often exactly what you're trying to prove), the math changes entirely — and the "it's all random" argument collapses even faster.
