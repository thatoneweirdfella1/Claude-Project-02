Great question! There were actually a few reasons, but the biggest one was cultural stigma rooted in painting traditions.

Smiling was associated with madness, lewdness, loudness, and drunkenness — states of being that were considered indecorous and undignified. In other words, if you were grinning broadly in a portrait, people assumed something was wrong with you.

Even in old paintings, wide smiles were often associated with madness, drunkenness, or otherwise informal, immature behavior. When photography came along, people simply carried those same social norms over.

Photographers quite literally instructed subjects to "say prunes" rather than "say cheese," to make their mouths look smaller — because small mouths were considered more beautiful, and big smiles were linked with mental instability.

There were also a couple of secondary reasons:

- **Long exposure times** — early cameras required subjects to hold perfectly still for many seconds or even minutes, and a smile is hard to maintain without turning into a weird grimace.
- **Rarity and formality** — photography was so expensive and uncommon that people knew this might be the only photograph ever made of them, so they opted to look thoughtful and serious, carrying over formal conventions from painted portraiture.

It wasn't really until the 1920s–1950s, as cameras became cheaper and more casual, that smiling became the norm we know today.
