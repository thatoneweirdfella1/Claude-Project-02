Haldon's twenty sixteen book, The Empire That Would Not Die, is the current state of the art and it widens the causal story far past simplification. The setup: the empire lost three quarters of its lands and probably more of its tax revenues, its towns turned to fortresses, its population decimated by warfare and plague, and yet it survived. One piece of Tainter that fully survives and matters for your framework: Byzantium and the Ottomans gradually lost power to competitors with no sudden loss of complexity, because collapse occurs and can only occur in a power vacuum, and where a strong competitor exists there can be no collapse. That's load-bearing for the modern case, since a peer-polity world forbids clean collapse and forces the slow-decline scenario where parallel building has time to work.

**Other cases**

Thin. Tainter's own language, truly rare, only example, is the field's position. Tikopia and similar island cases are real but involve a few thousand people, which puts them in Ostrom's bracket, not Tainter's. At state scale the documented set of survived major simplifications is essentially one.

**What this does to the framework**

The anchor holds for the claim you actually need: a complex state can lose three quarters of its territory and revenue, radically simplify its fiscal and military structure, and come out the other side functional for centuries. That's proven, once.

What's not proven, anywhere, is the planning version. No state in the record looked at its decline curve, chose simplification in advance, and executed a designed descent. Byzantium improvised under existential pressure and the improvisation happened to work, helped by mountains, plague hitting the enemy too, and an emperor-as-religion cohesion machine. So the honest reformulation for anything you publish: managed descent is documented as survivable, not as schedulable. The historical record supports adaptation capacity, not foresight.
