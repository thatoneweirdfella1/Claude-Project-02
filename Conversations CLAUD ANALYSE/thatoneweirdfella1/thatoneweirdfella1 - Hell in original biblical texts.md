**THE SHORT VERSION**

The word "hell" — as in eternal fiery torture forever — does not appear in the original texts. **THE WORD-BY-WORD BREAKDOWN**

Four completely different words got collapsed into one English word: "hell."

**Sheol** (Hebrew, Old Testament) — appears 65 times. Literally means "the grave" or "the pit." Where everyone goes when they die — righteous and wicked alike. In the KJV, out of 65 instances, it was translated "hell" 31 times and "the grave" the other 34 times — for the exact same word. That inconsistency wasn't accidental. Translators recoiled from saying good people went to "Sheol," so they translated it as "the grave" in passages about righteous people and "hell" in passages about wicked ones — same word, two different English translations based on editorial bias.

**Hades** (Greek, New Testament) — the direct equivalent of the Hebrew word Sheol. It means the grave or the pit, not a torture chamber. The added meaning of "underworld" or "place of departed spirits suffering" came from pagan Greek mythology, not from the biblical text itself.

**Tartarus** — the actual Greek word for "hell." It appears exactly once in the entire Bible, in 2 Peter 2:4, and it's about fallen angels awaiting judgment — not humans, not eternal, not torture. That's it. The one place they used the actual Greek word for hell, it's for angels, not people.

**Gehenna** — this one's different. It was a real physical place: the Valley of Hinnom outside Jerusalem, where garbage and bodies were burned continuously. Jesus used this word 11 of the 12 times it appears in the New Testament — and he used it specifically, not interchangeably with Hades, to make a particular point. It was a metaphor for destruction, not eternal conscious torture.

**WHERE ETERNAL TORMENT ACTUALLY CAME FROM**

The concept of eternal torment in hell is nowhere to be found in the original Hebrew and Greek texts. It is found in the writings of ancient Egyptians, Greeks, and Romans.

Here's the chain of how it got in:

Tertullian (160-220 AD) was one of the earliest Christian writers to embrace eternal torment. He was so influenced by pagan Greek philosophy that he directly quoted Plato in his writings: "Every soul is immortal." The immortal soul concept is a Greek idea — not a Hebrew one. The original Hebrew framework was that humans don't have immortal souls. They *are* souls. Death is death. Resurrection is the restoration. That's a fundamentally different framework.

The idea of eternal torment comes primarily from Greek thought — a merging of mythology and the belief in an immortal soul. For five centuries, Christian doctrine remained unaffected by hell until it was pushed in by Latin theologians like Tertullian and Augustine.

Then Jerome gets tasked with translating the whole Bible into Latin in the late 300s AD. Jerome imported pagan concepts of punishment, borrowing imagery from Greco-Roman myth and forcing it into scripture. Words like Gehenna, Hades, and Tartarus — each distinct in the Greek — were collapsed under a single Latin word that later hardened into the concept of "hell."

Augustine then codified it as official doctrine around 400-430 AD. The theologians who pushed eternal conscious torment — Augustine, Tertullian, Cyprian — all came from a North African Latin-speaking background with pagan religious roots that already taught eternal torment. The five schools that rejected eternal torment were Greek-speaking and recognized the original Greek context of the New Testament.

The doctrine of eternal torment was a prime tool for controlling the average churchgoer with fear, and it was congruent with secular mythologies of the time. Later, pop culture reinforced it through works like Dante's Inferno.

**WHAT THE ORIGINAL TEXTS ACTUALLY SAY ABOUT THE END**

**Destruction, not torture.** The original Hebrew framework is annihilation — the wicked simply cease to exist. Not eternal burning. The Lake of Fire in Revelation is described as "the second death" — death being the endpoint, not eternal screaming.

**Separation.** Some strands describe being cut off from God's presence as the consequence. Not fire. Absence.

**Judgment and accountability.** Yes — that's real across all the original texts including Enoch and Jubilees. There's a reckoning. But the Watchers are *bound*, then destroyed. Not tortured endlessly.

**Resurrection.** The original framework centers on resurrection and judgment — people are raised, judged, and either restored or ended. The Greek "immortal soul burning forever" construct isn't there.
