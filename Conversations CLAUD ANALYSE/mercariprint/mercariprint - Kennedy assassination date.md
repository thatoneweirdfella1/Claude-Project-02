**ASSISTANT:**
 Lyndon B. Johnson left office on **January 20, 1969**, when Richard Nixon was inaugurated as his successor.

What makes LBJ's departure particularly interesting from a historical standpoint is that he *chose* not to seek re-election. In March 1968, he made a stunning announcement that he would not run for another term — a decision driven largely by the enormous political pressure he faced over the Vietnam War, which had become deeply unpopular. His approval ratings had collapsed, and he faced serious primary challenges from anti-war Democrats like Eugene McCarthy and Robert F. Kennedy.

It's worth thinking about the arc of his presidency in context: Johnson had come to power under the most tragic of circumstances — sworn in on Air Force One just hours after Kennedy's assassination in Dallas (the very event you asked about). He then won the 1964 election in a historic landslide, went on to pass sweeping legislation like the Civil Rights Act, the Voting Rights Act, and Medicare as part of his "Great Society" vision — achievements many historians rank among the most consequential of the 20th century. Yet his legacy became deeply complicated by Vietnam, which essentially consumed his second term and drove him from office.

It's a fascinating case of how a presidency can be simultaneously transformative and tragic.
