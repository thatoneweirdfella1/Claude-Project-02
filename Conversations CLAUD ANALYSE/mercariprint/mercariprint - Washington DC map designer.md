**ASSISTANT:**
 The street layout and urban plan for Washington, D.C. was originally designed by **Pierre Charles L'Enfant**, a French-born engineer and architect who served in the American Revolutionary War. In 1791, President George Washington commissioned him to plan the new capital city.

L'Enfant's design was notable for its grand diagonal avenues (named after states) overlaid on a grid system, with open spaces, circles, and squares at major intersections — heavily influenced by European city planning, particularly Paris. The plan centered on the Capitol Building and the President's House (now the White House) as the two focal points of the city.

After L'Enfant was dismissed in 1792 due to conflicts with city commissioners, surveyor **Andrew Ellicott** (who had worked alongside L'Enfant) revised and finalized the plan for publication, with assistance from **Benjamin Banneker**, a free Black mathematician and astronomer.

**Ellicott's Revised Plan (1792)** is the version that was actually published and used for construction. It's also available through the Library of Congress and was the first widely circulated printed map of the capital.

The Library of Congress website (loc.gov) is your best bet — just search "L'Enfant Plan" or "Ellicott Plan" in their online map collection. You can also see physical reproductions at the **Library of Congress** in Washington, D.C. itself, or at the **National Archives**, if you ever visit in person.

**ASSISTANT:**
 Yes, there are some interesting discrepancies and alternative accounts surrounding L'Enfant's dismissal.

The **official reason** given was that he was insubordinate and difficult to work with — most notably, he demolished a house being built by Daniel Carroll (a prominent commissioner) that encroached on a planned street, without authorization. He also repeatedly refused to submit his plans to the three commissioners overseeing the project, insisting on reporting only directly to President Washington.

However, **L'Enfant himself** maintained that his dismissal was more about politics and power than his conduct. He felt the commissioners were trying to undermine his vision and authority over the project, and that they wanted control over the plan so they could benefit from land speculation — knowing in advance which areas would be developed and valuable. He believed his refusal to hand over the plans was justified because he feared they would be altered or exploited for personal financial gain.

L'Enfant also spent decades afterward petitioning Congress for compensation he felt he was owed, suggesting he saw himself as wronged rather than at fault. He was eventually offered payment but it was far less than he demanded.

So essentially, L'Enfant framed the conflict as one of **corruption and self-interest on the part of the commissioners**, while the official narrative focused on his **temperament and insubordination**. Whether his suspicions about land speculation were fully justified remains a matter of historical debate.
