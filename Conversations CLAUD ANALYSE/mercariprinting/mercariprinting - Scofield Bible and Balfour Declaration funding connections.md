## The Temple Institute (Machon HaMikdash)

### Founder and Origin

The Temple Institute, known in Hebrew as *Machon HaMikdash*, was founded in 1987 and is located in the Jewish Quarter of Jerusalem's Old City.

Its founder and head is Rabbi Yisrael Ariel, who served in the paratrooper brigade that took the Temple Mount during the Six Day War of 1967 and was one of the first soldiers to reach the Mount. The founding twenty years later was a direct outgrowth of that experience.

The very first night the Temple Mount returned to Israeli sovereignty, Rabbi Ariel was assigned to guard the Dome of the Rock — the site he regards as the location of the Holy of Holies. He later described that moment as a harbinger for the eventual birth of the Temple Institute.

In a 2015 interview, he explained his thinking in his own words: "I thought that a real military rabbinate should have in its storehouse what we have in the institute today: the Menorah, vessels for the Temple, clothes for the Kohanim (priests), and the altar, already in crates in the basement so that when we reach Jerusalem there will be trucks that unload everything, and we can get to work. So it's true that we usually improvise after something has happened, but in this case, we did not even have the beginning of what was needed. I asked myself why that was, and that is how the first ideas were born."

Rabbi Ariel was born in 1939, raised in Jerusalem, and studied at the Mercaz HaRav Yeshiva. He later served as Rabbi of the Jezreel Valley Regional Council, as a military rabbi during the Yom Kippur War, and as a prominent rabbi of Yamit in the Sinai before its evacuation under the peace accords with Egypt.

---

### Stated Mission

The Institute states its goals in two tiers:

The Temple Institute's ultimate goal is to see Israel rebuild the Holy Temple on Mount Moriah in Jerusalem, in accord with the Biblical commandments. Its short-term goal is to rekindle the flame of the Holy Temple in the hearts of mankind through education. Its long-term goal is to do all in its limited power to bring about the building of the Holy Temple.

It is a government-recognized, registered non-profit charitable organization in both Israel and the USA (501(c)(3)).

The Institute distinguishes itself from mainstream Orthodox Judaism on a key theological point: it holds that the Temple can and should be rebuilt by human hands, as opposed to those who believe we must wait for the Messiah to arrive before it can be rebuilt.

In mainstream Orthodox Judaism, the rebuilding of the Temple is generally left to the coming of the Jewish Messiah and to divine providence. The Temple Institute represents a minority position among Orthodox Jews who want to realize the construction of a Third Temple in present times.

---

### What It Has Built and Prepared

The Institute's most concrete and documented output is a collection of functional sacred vessels and priestly garments, made to biblical specification and displayed in its Jerusalem visitors' center:

After many years of effort and toil, the Institute has completed the three most important and central vessels of the Divine service: the seven-branched candelabra (Menorah), made of pure gold; the golden Incense Altar; and the golden Table of the Showbread. These vessels are made according to the exact specifications of the Bible from original source materials such as gold, copper, silver, and wood. These are described as authentic, functional vessels — not merely replicas or models — fit and ready for use in the service of the Holy Temple.

As of 2008, a major project was the creation of sacred uniforms for the High Priest (*Kohen Gadol*) and ordinary priests. The High Priest's breastplate (*Hoshen*) and *Ephod* have been completed. The *Tzitz* — the High Priest's golden crown — was completed in 2007. Silver trumpets for use in Temple service have also been made.

**The Red Heifer project:** Numbers 19 in the Torah prescribes that the ashes of a ritually pure red heifer are required for purification before Temple service can begin. The Institute has attempted to locate a *parah adumah* (red heifer) consistent with the requirements for purposes of purification necessary to enter the Temple sanctuary. It had previously identified two candidates — one in 1997 and another in 2002 — that it initially declared kosher, but later found each to be unsuitable. In September 2022, five unblemished red heifers arrived in Israel from the USA and were found by inspecting rabbis to meet the qualifications. They are being fed and cared for until the time they can be used to create the necessary ashes for purification.

**Education and training:** The Temple Institute runs a full-time yeshiva for high school-aged boys in the Jewish Quarter, with a curriculum emphasizing Temple-related studies, headed by Rabbi Yisrael Ariel and his son Rabbi Azaria Ariel. The Institute has also created a Levitical Choir, training singers to perform the Temple hymns, with apprentice choristers coming from across Israel to study ancient liturgical material.

**Temple Mount pilgrimages:** The Institute conducts organized *aliyot* (ascents) to the Temple Mount in accordance with halachic requirements, during weekday mornings — the only hours Jews are presently permitted to visit the Mount under Israeli government policy that cedes administrative authority to the Muslim Waqf.

### Political and Geopolitical Context

The Institute's goal is explicitly incompatible with the current status quo: its representatives have acknowledged it would be impossible to construct the Third Temple anywhere but the Temple Mount compound — the same site that houses Al-Aqsa Mosque, the third holiest site in Islam — while declining to specify what would become of the mosque.

The Institute is a registered Israeli nonprofit and has received awards from the Israeli Ministry of Education, making it a recognized institution within Israeli civil society, even as its ultimate goal remains one of the most geopolitically sensitive propositions in the world.

## The 2004 Re-establishment of the Sanhedrin

### Historical Context and the Maimonidean Framework

The Sanhedrin — the 71-member supreme court of Jewish law — had last convened in Tiberias around 425 CE, when the Roman Emperor Theodosius II suppressed the Jewish patriarchate. The Sanhedrin's final dissolution is usually dated to 358 CE when Hillel II fixed the Jewish calendar mathematically, eliminating the need for the body to announce the new month — its last major institutional act.

The theological and legal path to reconstitution was laid by Maimonides (1135–1204), who argued in his *Mishneh Torah* that even though the chain of *semikhah* (rabbinic ordination from Moses) had been broken, it could be restored if sages in the Land of Israel agreed by consensus on a single candidate — who would then have valid ordination and could pass it to others, reconstituting the body. The 2004 attempt is considered the sixth in recent history, modeled after Rabbi Jacob Berab's attempt in 1538, and following attempts in 1830, 1901, 1940, and 1949.

---

### The Founding: October 2004, Tiberias

In October 2004 (Tishrei 5765), a group of rabbis representing varied Orthodox communities in Israel undertook a ceremony in Tiberias — the city where the original Sanhedrin had last met — with about one hundred of them having proper *semikhah*. This was one year after the re-establishment of *semikhah*. A *Beit Din* (court) of 71 was formed.

It was Rabbi Tzvi Idan who gathered over 100 rabbis in Tiberias in October 2004 to formally re-establish the Sanhedrin. He ordained 70 rabbis and was appointed as temporary *Nasi* (president).

The procedure: the Sanhedrin was re-established through the ordination of one rabbi agreed upon by many prominent rabbis in Israel and approved as "fitting to serve" by former Chief Sephardi Rabbi Ovadiah Yosef and leading Ashkenazi Rabbi Yosef Shalom Elyashiv. That rabbi, then considered to have received authentic ordination as handed down from Moses, was then able to give ordination to 70 others, making up the quorum of 71 necessary for a functioning Sanhedrin.

The first individual selected to receive *semikhah* by consensus was Rabbi Moshe Halberstam, a relatively well-known and widely respected figure — the *rosh yeshiva* of the Tshokava Yeshiva in Jerusalem and a prominent member of the Edah Charedis rabbinical court.

The choice of Tiberias was deliberate: it seemed fitting to reconvene in the city where the Sanhedrin had last met, with the understanding that the body would eventually relocate to Jerusalem.

---

### Key Figures

**Rabbi Tzvi Idan** — Organizer and first interim *Nasi*; author of *Asot Mishpat*, a legal work on the laws of re-establishing the Sanhedrin. He led the outreach campaign, reportedly distributing over 50,000 pamphlets to rabbis across Israel.

**Rabbi Adin Steinsaltz** — About a year after the founding, the Sanhedrin unanimously selected Rabbi Adin Steinsaltz, one of the most celebrated Talmudic scholars of the 20th century, as the new *Nasi*. He accepted reluctantly, saying he would only be a transitional figure. He requested the body be referred to modestly as the "nascent Sanhedrin" or "developing Sanhedrin" rather than simply "the Sanhedrin," reflecting his caution about premature claims of full legitimacy. He ultimately resigned in 2008. Steinsaltz died in 2020.

**Rabbi Yisrael Ariel** — Founder of the Temple Institute and a member of the Sanhedrin, serving at various points as a leading voice. Haredi Zionist rabbis involved in the revival included Yisrael Ariel and Yoel Schwartz.

---

### Stated Goals and Self-Conception

The organization regarded itself as a provisional body awaiting integration into the Israeli government as both a supreme court and an upper house of the Knesset.

From the Maimonidean theological tradition it drew upon, the re-establishment of the Sanhedrin is described as a *precondition* for, not a consequence of, the coming of the Messiah — a significant point of dispute within Orthodoxy. Maimonides wrote: "I am of the opinion that the Sanhedrin will return before the advent of the Messiah, and this will be one of the signs."

The Sanhedrin's spokesmen said that due to concerns that external pressure would be brought to bear upon individuals not to take part, the names of most participants would not be made public.

---

### Reception and Controversy

The body was not broadly recognized. It was mostly ignored by the Haredi community and stirred debate in both religious and secularist circles. The Israeli secular press regarded it as an illegitimate fundamentalist organization of rabbis. The Lithuanian Haredi *Yated* newspaper ran several articles condemning Rabbi Yisrael Ariel and his Temple Institute using the expression "poisonous opinions."

As of June 2023, that effort is officially ongoing, supported mainly by the Temple Institute, although practically defunct. There has not been a "full meeting" of the Sanhedrin since 2005.

---

### What It Has Done Since 2004

Despite its limited institutional recognition, the nascent Sanhedrin has issued a substantial number of rulings, statements, and actions over two decades:

**Temple Mount and Third Temple (2004–2005):** In the first year of operation, the Sanhedrin was primarily involved with discussions about the Temple Mount, forming a committee to determine its exact halachic location. One of the first actions was an attempted ascent to the Temple Mount on December 6, 2004, after receiving approval from Israeli police — the group was told it could only enter in groups of ten, so the effort was postponed.

**Gaza Disengagement (2005):** The National Religious camp took great interest in the Sanhedrin with the hope it might prevent disengagement from Gaza. The Sanhedrin came out with several strongly worded rulings against "disengagement," advocated non-violent protests, but fell short of condoning stronger protest.

**Military and civil disobedience (2006):** The Sanhedrin issued repeated calls for soldiers and policemen to disobey orders it regarded as contrary to Jewish law. It summoned an IDF officer who had issued restraining orders against West Bank residents to appear before the court. It opposed the Gay Pride parade in Jerusalem in 2006, declaring participation in security operations for the parade a criminal act. Under Israeli laws governing "incitement," this action was potentially illegal, and Rabbi Yisrael Ariel was arrested and briefly brought in for questioning.

**Shofar on Shabbat (2006):** The Sanhedrin blew the shofar on Rosh Hashanah in September 2006, which fell on a Shabbat — a practice normally prohibited absent a functioning Sanhedrin. This was essentially a claim to the rights and authority of a true Sanhedrin.

**Passover sacrifice campaigns (2007–2008):** The Sanhedrin made repeated requests to the Israeli Supreme Court and Prime Minister to allow a Passover sacrifice on the Temple Mount, and conducted conferences on the *Korban Pesach* (Passover offering). It also petitioned to set up a minimal altar on the Temple Mount in preparation for Passover.

**Appointment of a High Priest (2016):** In August 2016, the nascent Sanhedrin selected Rabbi Baruch Kahane as candidate for the next *Kohen Gadol* (High Priest). The selection was made as a precaution for Yom Kippur — the reasoning being that if political conditions should suddenly change and Jews gain access to the Temple Mount, Torah law would require the sacrifices to begin immediately, necessitating a prepared High Priest candidate. Rabbi Kahane stated that if access were granted, Temple service could begin in less than one week.

**Altar consecration and sacrifice reenactment (December 2018):** The Sanhedrin ordered a stone altar built — nine feet square and five feet high, constructed of aerated concrete ruled fit for Temple use — and dedicated it on the last day of Hanukkah just outside the walls of the Old City of Jerusalem. A full-dress reenactment of the daily sacrifice was held, and invitations were sent to representatives of 70 nations to attend.

**70 Nations Conference (September 2019):** The Sanhedrin hosted a conference of an "Organization of 70 Nations" in Israel, inviting representatives of nations to participate in a framework based on the Seven Noahide Laws. The conference culminated in an animal sacrifice on the Mount of Olives, described as a reenactment of Noah's covenant sacrifice.

**Red Heifer coordination (ongoing through 2025):** As of November 2025, the nascent Sanhedrin issued a formal ruling coordinating with the Temple Institute on the red heifer question, confirming that a July 2025 burning of a red heifer calf was not a qualified ceremony under Jewish law and that no purification for Temple service was achieved — while affirming the ongoing preparation process.

---

### Summary Assessment

The 2004 Sanhedrin is a documented religious institution — registered, publishing rulings in Hebrew and English, with an identifiable membership, named leaders, and a traceable history of actions. It has no recognized legal standing within the Israeli state and has been rejected or ignored by most of mainstream Orthodoxy. However, it represents an interlocking network with the Temple Institute (Rabbi Yisrael Ariel is a member of both), and together the two organizations have systematically advanced preparations — vessels, garments, priestly training, altar construction, red heifer procurement, and High Priest selection — for what they describe as an imminent resumption of Temple service.

## The Reece Committee: Background, Findings, and Fate

### Predecessor: The Cox Committee (1952)

The Reece Committee did not emerge in a vacuum. In April 1952, the Select Committee to Investigate Tax-Exempt Foundations, led by E. Eugene Cox of Georgia, began an investigation of "educational and philanthropic foundations and other comparable organizations" exempt from taxation. In the fall of 1952, all foundations with assets of $10 million or more received a questionnaire. The foundations cooperated willingly. The Cox Committee's final report, submitted to Congress in January 1953, endorsed the loyalty of the foundations: "So far as we can ascertain, there is little basis for the belief expressed in some quarters that foundation funds are being diverted from their intended use."

Unhappy with the Cox Committee's conclusions, Rep. B. Carroll Reece pushed for a continuation of its work. In April 1954, the House authorized the Reece Committee. Unlike its predecessor, which limited its attention to generalities, the Reece Committee mounted a comprehensive inquiry into both the motives for establishing foundations and their influence on public life.

---

### Structure of the Reece Committee

The Reece Committee's investigative inquiry was headed by Norman Dodd, a former banker appointed as Director of Research. The select committee comprised three Republicans and two Democrats. The Republicans were Reece, Jesse P. Wolcott of Michigan, and Angier L. Goodwin of Massachusetts. The two Democrats were Wayne L. Hays of Ohio and Gracie Pfost of Idaho. The committee's general counsel was René Wormser, who would later write the most comprehensive account of the investigation.

The committee was authorized by the House to investigate whether tax-exempt foundations were using their resources for "un-American and subversive activities; political purposes; propaganda or attempts to influence legislation."

---

### The Carnegie Endowment Minutes: What Dodd Found

One of the most consequential discoveries of the investigation came through a straightforward archival review. Norman Dodd's research staff sent questionnaires to major foundations. Joseph Johnson, then president of the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace (and successor to Alger Hiss, who had been exposed as a Soviet agent), invited Dodd to send a staff member to examine the Endowment's trustee minutes — records that had been boxed away in storage and which Johnson apparently had not reviewed.

What Dodd's researcher Katherine Casey found in those minutes, as Dodd later described it in a 1982 interview with G. Edward Griffin: beginning in 1908, when the Carnegie Foundation began operations, the trustees discussed a single question throughout that year: "Is there any means known more effective than war, assuming you wish to alter the life of an entire people?" The trustees concluded that no means more effective than war was known. They then turned, in 1909, to the question of how to involve the United States in a war.

Dodd further described what the minutes showed happened after World War I: the Carnegie Endowment trustees reasoned that if they could get control of education in the United States, they would be able to prevent a return to the way of life as it had been prior to the war. They recruited the Rockefeller Foundation to assist. According to Dodd's Reece Committee report: "They divided the task in parts, giving to the Rockefeller Foundation the responsibility of altering education as it pertains to domestic subjects, but Carnegie retained the task of altering our education in foreign affairs and about international relations."

The minutes also showed, per Dodd, that when the foundations tried to recruit prominent American historians — including Charles and Mary Beard — to alter how they presented American history, and were refused, they decided they needed to "build our own stable of historians," approaching the Guggenheim Foundation, which specialized in fellowships, to identify and fund doctoral candidates in American History who were of "the right caliber."

**Important evidentiary note:** The Carnegie minutes themselves are primary-source documents. Dodd's account of their contents is relayed through his interviews decades later. The committee's formal report does reference the education-division finding, and Wormser's 1958 book *Foundations: Their Power and Influence* discusses these points in detail, but the specific quotations from the minutes come primarily from Dodd's later recollections rather than from published committee transcripts.

---

### The Rowan Gaither Conversation

The other major episode that has become a focal point in accounts of this investigation was an off-the-record conversation between Dodd and H. Rowan Gaither, Jr., president of the Ford Foundation. In Dodd's own words from the 1982 Griffin interview: Gaither said: "Mr. Dodd, all of us who have a hand in the making of policies here have had experience either with the OSS during the war, or with European economic administration after the war." Gaither then, according to Dodd, voluntarily disclosed that the Foundation operated under directives — described as emanating from the White House — whose substance was "to use our grant-making power to alter life in the United States so that it can be comfortably merged with the Soviet Union."

Gaither's public congressional testimony, by contrast, was the opposite: "The Ford Foundation has not used any of its resources for un-American or subversive activities. Nor have we used any of our resources for political purposes, propaganda, or attempts to influence legislation," Gaither testified.

**Evidentiary note:** The Gaither statement is Dodd's account of a private, off-the-record conversation. No independent corroboration exists. Dodd related it consistently over decades, including under his own name, but it remains a single-witness account of a private exchange.

---

### What the Official Majority Report Concluded

The committee's formal majority report (House Report No. 2681, 83rd Congress, 2nd Session, filed December 1954) was signed by the three Republicans. Its documented conclusions included:

The Dodd report defined "subversive" as "any action having as its purpose the alteration of either the principle or the form of the United States Government by other than constitutional means." It argued that the Ford Foundation, Rockefeller Foundation, and Carnegie Endowment were using funds excessively on projects at Columbia, Harvard, the University of Chicago, and the University of California in order to enable "oligarchical collectivism."

The report conceded that "with several exceptions such as the Institute of Pacific Relations, foundations have not directly supported organizations which, in turn, operated to support communism." However, it concluded that some of the larger foundations had directly supported "subversion" in the sense of undermining "some of our vitally protective concepts and principles."

Regarding foreign policy, the report stated: "In the international field, foundations, and an interlock among some of them and certain intermediary organizations, have exercised a strong effect upon our foreign policy and upon public education in things international."

The committee's general counsel René Wormser later summarized the concentrated-power finding in his 1958 book: "An unparalleled amount of power is concentrated increasingly in the hands of an interlocking and self-perpetuating group. Unlike the power of corporate management, it is unchecked by stockholders; unlike the power of government, it is unchecked by the people; unlike the power of churches, it is unchecked by any firmly established canons of value."

The committee specifically found that the Council on Foreign Relations was part of this interlock, "financed both by the Rockefeller and Carnegie" foundations, and "overwhelmingly propagandizes the globalist concept."

Although the promotion of internationalism and moral relativism by foundations concerned the committee, it saw their concentrated power as the more central threat — arguing that even if the power were exercised benignly, it posed a structural threat to democratic government.

---

### How the Investigation Was Terminated

The Reece Committee's report was submitted in the midst of the ultimately successful efforts to censure Senator Joseph McCarthy. McCarthy's fall led to a discrediting of all efforts that "smacked of redbaiting," and the report failed to attract much attention.

The more immediate termination of the *hearings* — before the report was filed — came from internal sabotage. The hearings conducted by the select committee were sabotaged by its ranking minority member, Democrat congressman Wayne L. Hays of Ohio. Hays initially promised to fully support Dodd's work, then persistently interrupted witnesses in public hearings. His disruptive behavior was not fully contained by chairman Reece.

According to Dodd, Hays became convinced he was being double-crossed, put on a show in the public hearing room, and the hearings were terminated. Carroll Reece made the decision to halt further hearings to prevent further sabotage.

Dodd also described in his interviews that a White House liaison — identified only as "Major Person" — visited Hays multiple times asking him to break up the investigation, though Hays told Dodd he refused those entreaties before eventually turning against the committee for other reasons.

Hays later apologized to Carroll Reece and Norman Dodd, but the investigation was not resumed.

The committee's two Democrats, Wayne Hays and Gracie Pfost, refused to sign the final report. Republican Angier Goodwin signed with strong reservations, filing a supplementary statement that agreed with the diametrically opposite conclusions of the Cox Committee.

Subsequent funding was not forthcoming for the second year of the investigations, and the report was filed December 16, 1954.

---

### What Happened to It Afterward

The investigation produced no policy consequences. The committee shed light on the big foundations' promotion of empiricism, centralized team research, big universities over small colleges, moral relativism, internationalism, and social engineering — but the effort ran aground on accusations of intellectual McCarthyism and the disruptive antics of Wayne Hays.

Wormser published *Foundations: Their Power and Influence* in 1958, the most complete account written by a committee participant. The book argued that "the far-reaching power of the large foundations and of the interlock has so influenced the press, the radio, and even the government that it has become extremely difficult for objective criticism of foundation practices to get into news channels without having first been distorted, slanted, discredited, and at times ridiculed."

Dodd gave several recorded interviews in his later years, most notably the 1982 interview with G. Edward Griffin of the John Birch Society, which became the primary vehicle through which the committee's findings reached later audiences. Dodd died in January 1987. Senator Jesse Helms later promised to resume the investigation, according to those familiar with Dodd, but never did.

The full hearing transcripts are publicly available as a Government Printing Office document: *Tax-Exempt Foundations: Hearings, 83rd Congress, 2nd Session* (Washington: GPO, 1954), and the official report exists in the congressional record as Union Calendar No. 926, Report No. 2681.
