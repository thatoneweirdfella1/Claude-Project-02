 You're talking about **attachment theory** and how early childhood attachment patterns (particularly with primary caregivers like mothers) shape adult romantic relationships.

Here's what you're remembering:

**The core concept:**

People with insecure attachment styles—often stemming from inconsistent, neglectful, or emotionally unavailable parenting—enter adult relationships with unmet emotional needs from childhood. They unconsciously seek partners to fill these voids.

**Specifically:**

1. **Seeking the nurture they never received**: People try to get from romantic partners what they missed in childhood—consistent affection, validation, emotional safety, unconditional acceptance. They're essentially trying to heal childhood wounds through adult relationships.

2. **External validation replacing self-love**: When someone lacks self-worth or self-love (often because they weren't adequately mirrored, validated, or loved as children), they become dependent on their partner's love to feel okay about themselves. Their partner's affection becomes a substitute for the internal sense of worth they never developed.

**The psychological terms:**

- **Anxious attachment**: Fear of abandonment, constant need for reassurance, hypersensitivity to partner's availability
- **Avoidant attachment**: Discomfort with intimacy, emotional distance, self-reliance as a defense mechanism
- **Disorganized attachment**: Conflicting desires for closeness and distance, often from traumatic early experiences

**The problem:**

No partner can successfully fill the void left by childhood neglect or replace genuine self-love. This creates dysfunctional patterns—codependency, emotional volatility, repeated relationship failures—because the person is asking their partner to do something impossible: retroactively parent them or become their sole source of worth.

## **TOP 10 REASONS FOR SUICIDE**

**1. Mental Health Disorders**
- Depression and alcohol use disorders are the most relevant risk factors, with anxiety, personality disorders, eating disorders, trauma-related disorders, and psychosis also contributing
- Mental illness is present 27% to more than 90% of the time at suicide
- Half of all people who die by suicide may have major depressive disorder

**2. Substance Abuse**
- Alcohol and drug use disorders significantly increase risk
- Depression causes over two-thirds of the 30,000 reported suicides in the U.S. annually

**3. Relationship Problems/Loss**
- 45.1% of suicide decedents without known psychiatric conditions experienced relationship problems/loss, including intimate partner problems (30.2%) and arguments/conflicts (17.5%)

**4. Physical Health Problems**
- Cancer diagnosis approximately doubles the subsequent frequency of suicide
- Chronic pain, traumatic brain injury, kidney failure, HIV, and systemic lupus increase risk
- In Japan, health problems are listed as the primary justification for suicide

**5. Economic/Financial Crisis**
- Suicide has been associated with tough economic conditions, including unemployment
- Loss of job, financial hardship, poverty

**6. Legal Issues**
- Legal problems are a significant life stressor related to suicides in individuals without psychiatric disorders

**7. Loss of Home/Housing Instability**
- Loss of home identified as a major stressor

**8. Social Isolation/Lack of Support**
- Lack of social support is a key factor
- Loneliness, disconnection from community

**9. Previous Suicide Attempt**
- Those hospitalized for suicidal behavior have an 8.6% lifetime risk of suicide
- 40% of persons who complete suicide made a previous attempt

**10. Trauma/Adverse Experiences**
- Child abuse and neglect
- Adverse childhood experiences
- Violence exposure
- PTSD

**Additional factors:**
- Personality traits such as impulsivity, aggression, neuroticism, anger, suspiciousness and perfectionism
- Sleep disturbances like insomnia and sleep apnea are risk factors independent of depression
- Access to lethal means (firearms, pesticides)

## **GENDER DISPARITIES IN SUPPORT SERVICES**

**Men have significantly less access/utilization:**

**Mental health service utilization:**
- 72-89% of women who died by suicide had contact with mental health professionals at some point in their life, compared to 41-58% of men
- In the year before suicide, only 19.7% of men had contact with mental health professionals compared to 35.0% of women
- In Canada, men were 2.7 times less likely to utilize mental health services than women
- Only 36% of referrals to NHS talking therapies in the UK are for men
- In 2021, just 40% of men with reported mental illness received mental health services compared to 52% of women

**Why men don't seek help:**
- Traditional masculinity traits include stoicism, invulnerability, and self-reliance, which don't fit comfortably with psychological help-seeking
- Men are more likely to ignore mental problems in early stages and prefer mental health specialists over general practitioners
- Men feel providers mislabel and underestimate their needs and don't have genuine interest in their problems
- Social stigma around male vulnerability
- Few courses and trainings focus on gender differences in mental health, leaving providers less equipped to serve men

**Diagnosis gaps:**
- Women are 13-21% more likely than men to receive a psychiatric affective diagnosis
- Men are diagnosed with depression at lower rates than women despite higher suicide rates, suggesting diagnostic tools need improvement
