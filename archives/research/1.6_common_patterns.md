# 1.6 Most Common Patterns Across the 10 Examples

The 4 patterns that appeared most frequently, with full analysis of how they're communicated, why they happen, and what AI gets wrong by default.

---

## Pattern 1 — Vague Pronoun With No Local Referent

**Frequency:** 7 / 10 examples

**How it's communicated:**
The user uses "it," "this," "that," "the X," or similar proxies without including the noun they refer to in the current message. Sometimes the referent exists in a prior turn or in an attached image. Sometimes it doesn't exist anywhere in the conversation.

Examples:
- "what number did *it* say the coincedences were at"
- "categorize *it* to where..."
- "Will *this* work snf..."
- "Wtf is *this*"
- "you can get a jist of what *we* are doing here"

**Why it happens:**
The user's working memory holds the referent as obvious — they know exactly what "it" is because they're thinking about it. The pronoun is a compression shortcut. For people with ADHD, expressing the shortcut and the full noun feels redundant in the moment.

**What AI does wrong by default:**
Requests clarification immediately ("What does 'it' refer to?") rather than attempting domain-anchored resolution first. In most cases, the referent can be inferred from: domain context of the message, prior conversation turns, or attached media. Asking for clarification when inference is possible breaks flow and signals low comprehension.

**Correct behavior:**
Attempt pronoun resolution using (1) prior turn context, (2) image/media content, (3) domain anchoring from other nouns in the message. Request clarification only if resolution confidence is < 0.5.

## Pattern 2 — Emotional Opener That Buries the Functional Request

**Frequency:** 6 / 10 examples

**How it's communicated:**
The message opens with frustration, fear, personal narrative, conspiracy framing, or roleplay language. The actual informational need is present but wrapped inside the emotional content. Sometimes the emotional content is the entire first sentence; the question only appears mid-message or at the very end.

Examples:
- "Wtf is this why does my apps menu have 5 ads on it now"
- "Okay so what's okay so cancer in the US is I guess I would fall into the Rockefeller medicine situation..."
- "You are a senior investigative journalist... MASTER INVESTIGATIVE REPORT PROMPT — FULL GOD-MODE..."
- "Explain briefly, please, how the mind of a based person seems to work in terms of When you're like, earlier in my life..."

**Why it happens:**
Emotional state precedes rational framing for most people — especially those with ADHD. The feeling arrives first, the structured question comes later (or doesn't). The user is not being irrational; they are expressing context that feels important to them, even if it's not what they technically need the AI to process.

Roleplay / conspiracy wrappers are a specific variant: the user has learned that framing a question "properly" may get a better or more serious response, so they add performative scaffolding.

**What AI does wrong by default:**
Responds to the emotional frame rather than the embedded request. Validates the frustration, engages with the conspiracy premise, or plays the assigned role — treating the wrapper as the content rather than stripping it to find the question underneath.

**Correct behavior:**
Run affect-stripping first. Identify whether the emotional content is the actual topic (e.g., "I'm overwhelmed and need help managing emotions") or a wrapper around a separate informational request. If it's a wrapper, extract the request. If it's the topic, route to emotional processing.

## Pattern 3 — Request Appears Only in the Final Clause

**Frequency:** 5 / 10 examples

**How it's communicated:**
The message is structured as: context → context → context → [actual question at the end]. The beginning of the message is often long, narrative, or explanatory. The actual imperative or question word only appears in the last sentence or two.

Examples:
- "...So explain briefly how that works in, like, a screenshot. Amount. Of words..."
- "...so one kind of ask is is what is a known remedy that they use outside of the US the cures cancer..."
- "...I just need some more to categorize it to where..."
- "...what cheap alternatives have the same chemical"

**Why it happens:**
ADHD communication often follows a stream-of-consciousness pattern where the user arrives at the request through narration. They need to "talk themselves to" the question. The question at the end is genuine; the content before it is how they got there.

Also: conversational habit. Many people open with justification before making a request, even in written form. The AI is treated like a person who needs context before being asked.

**What AI does wrong by default:**
Reads the message top-to-bottom and treats the opening content as the primary topic. Summarizes or responds to the setup without ever reaching the question buried at the end. Or, it picks up multiple possible questions from the middle of the message and ignores the explicit one at the end.

**Correct behavior:**
Scan from the end of the message first. Locate the most recent question mark, imperative verb, or "tell me / explain / give me" structure. Treat that as the primary request. Treat everything before it as context. Only deviate from this if the end-request is grammatically incomplete or contradicts an earlier explicit request.

## Pattern 4 — Typo or Autocorrect Changes Word Meaning

**Frequency:** 4 / 10 examples

**How it's communicated:**
One or more words in the message are corrupted — by phonetic autocorrect substitution, dropped letters, merged words, or swapped characters. The corruption is often a real word, making it harder to detect as a typo. The surrounding context is the only signal that something is wrong.

Examples:
- "unison hat" → "Unisom that"
- "thsat ion ly iuncludes claudes replioes. soim" → cascading typos across entire message
- "snf" → "and"
- "coincedences" → "coincidences"

**Why it happens:**
Fast typing, speech-to-text errors, autocorrect over-corrections, or ADHD-related motor/attention mismatch between thought speed and typing speed. The user's internal model of what they wrote is correct; the output is not.

**What AI does wrong by default:**
Treats the typo as literal. Looks up "unison hat" as if it's a product. Requests clarification on "snf." Fails to parse a message that is easily understood with phonetic reconstruction. This is a failure of robustness — humans handle this effortlessly; AI often doesn't.

**Correct behavior:**
Attempt phonetic/contextual reconstruction for any unrecognizable token. Check: does the reconstructed word fit the domain? Does the sentence become grammatically valid? If yes, proceed with the reconstruction and optionally note it in parentheses. Only ask for clarification if reconstruction confidence is < 0.6 and the word is load-bearing.

## Cross-Pattern Interactions

These four patterns frequently co-occur. The most difficult messages combine all four:

- Emotional opener (Pattern 2)
- Trailing into preamble (combined with Pattern 3)
- Vague pronouns throughout (Pattern 1)
- Typos scattered across (Pattern 4)

Example: The multi-boot USB message combines heavy typos + assumed shared context + no explicit question + continuation signal — a four-pattern collision that requires all four resolution strategies simultaneously.

The pipeline must handle co-occurrence, not treat each pattern as mutually exclusive.
