# 20.2 Clarifying Question Templates

Reusable question templates for the translator pipeline, derived from real instances in the 135-conversation archive where a clarifying question led to a demonstrably better answer.

---

## Source Examples

Seven conversations were examined where Claude asked a clarifying question mid-conversation and the user's answer changed the direction or quality of the response.

---

### Example 1 — Typo Corrupts Key Term (Product Lookup)

**Original input:**
> "Considering the ingredient in unison hat makes it so strong. What cheap alternatives have the same chemical"

**What was unclear:** "unison hat" is phonetic corruption of "Unisom SleepTabs" — but the active ingredient question is completely answerable once the product is identified.

**Clarifying question Claude asked:**
> "I think there might be a typo in your question. Are you asking about Unisom (the sleep aid medication)?"

**User's response:** "Yeah"

**How it improved the answer:**
Without the clarification, Claude would have either answered nothing (no product called "unison hat") or guessed wrong. With confirmation, Claude could explain the doxylamine succinate vs. diphenhydramine split across Unisom formulations and name exact generics by active ingredient. The question unlocked the entire answer; the answer was impossible without it.

---

### Example 2 — Unclear Intent: Build vs. Explore

**Original input:**
> "i want to create an app that does what ai language models, mainly claude, cant. id like it to bridge the gap between adhd and ai... i dont know how to go about this"

**What was unclear:** The input contained two entirely different requests embedded in a single ramble — (a) "explore whether this is possible and what it would look like" and (b) "begin building a technical architecture." Answering the wrong one would waste the entire conversation.

**Clarifying question Claude asked:**
> "Do you want to build this as a tool/wrapper app, or are you asking me to help you think through whether it's even possible and what it would look like? Because those are different conversations."

**User's response:** "whats smarter, and would help me more"

**How it improved the answer:**
Claude recognized the meta-question as a request for a recommendation, not a dodge, and gave a concrete reason (50-70 abandoned tasks = don't build before understanding the failure mode). The response became a structured exploration framework with 4 diagnostic questions. Without the goal-clarification question, Claude would have built technical architecture for a tool the user hadn't yet defined.

---

### Example 3 — Scope Too Large, Goal Unknown

**Original input:**
> Long rambling message about roommate conflict, ADHD, housing situation, past trauma, learned helplessness, specific incident about interruptions, and a draft message to send.

**Claude's first response addressed roughly 6% of the content.** User noticed:
> "And does that address all the issues because it looks as if you're focusing on 6 percent of what u were told"

**Clarifying question Claude asked:**
> "Before I write anything I need to know one thing. What's the goal here? Because that changes everything.
>
> Option A: You want him to actually understand what he's doing and why it's harmful.
> Option B: You want to hold your ground and make the point clearly without softening it for his comfort.
> Option C: Both — you want him to get it AND you're not willing to water it down.
>
> Which one?"

**User's response:** "Do it properly. Start by addressing what he says and what he does are two different things and how it's harmful. But end it in something positive and understanding so he's left not defensive."

**How it improved the answer:**
User's answer named the structure (confrontation → resolution), the tone (firm but not attacking), and the emotional goal (leave him not defensive). Claude then wrote a comprehensive 5-paragraph message addressing all 7 issues from the original input with correct priority order. Without the goal question, Claude was pattern-matching to the shortest legible piece of the input.

---

### Example 4 — Ambiguous Referent ("It")

**Original input:**
> "The gk fast rocket. What happens when it appears to suddenly stop"

**What was unclear:** "gk fast rocket" could be (a) GK Persei nova, (b) the Go Fast UAV video, or (c) a SpaceX/rocket launch video. Claude initially answered GK Persei (the star), then the Go Fast UAP video, both wrong.

**The clarifying exchange:**
User: "Uhh what. I asked about the go fast rocket"
Claude: answered UAP video
User: "What the fuck are u talking about"
Claude: "You literally said 'go fast rocket.' What are you actually asking about?"
User: "2014?"
Claude: "Which one do you mean? Tic Tac (2004), or one of the Roosevelt encounters (2014-2015)?"
User: "Dude I'm talking about how it went up and appears to hit the firmament"

**How it improved the answer:**
Only after "hit the firmament" did Claude finally understand the user was asking about a rocket launch video in the flat earth community — a completely different category of question. The correct answer (about the plasma exhaust behavior at altitude creating the appearance of a collision with an invisible barrier) required knowing the actual referent. Without resolving the vague pronoun chain, three consecutive answers were wasted.

---

### Example 5 — Format Ambiguity in Edit Request

**Original input:**
> "Fix only the parts you mentioned needing help. Leave the part about the grandmother. If it's your first two bullets and only correct the 3rd and final 4th bullet"

*[This came after Claude offered four editorial suggestions on a letter and suggested changes to specific sections.]*

**What made this clear:** This is actually an example where the user *provided* the scope constraint upfront, producing a clean answer. The contrast case (two turns earlier) was:

> "Analyze"

With only the letter uploaded and no scope defined, Claude did a full 4-point analysis when the user's real need turned out to be: correct typos + clarify one ambiguous line, leave everything else. The over-analysis produced suggestions the user had to sort through and reject.

The implicit clarifying question needed before "Analyze" was:
> "Are you looking for: (a) a full critique with suggested rewrites, (b) feedback on specific sections, or (c) only error correction while leaving your voice intact?"

### Example 6 — Missing Context for Technical Identification

**Original input:**
> "This has a diagnostic port on it I need to know what I need based on this model to access that port to gain diagnostic info on it"

*(with a photo of a product label, not the port)*

**What was unclear:** The product label shows the device model but not the diagnostic port type. Without seeing the physical connector, Claude couldn't recommend the right cable/tool.

**Clarifying question Claude asked:**
> "To help you identify what connector and tools you need, I need to see:
> 1. Physical location of the diagnostic port — Can you take a photo showing where on the device the diagnostic port is located?
> 2. Close-up of the port itself — What does it look like? (USB, serial, pogo pins, etc.)"

**How it improved the answer:**
The original image contained everything *except* the port. The clarifying question redirected the user's next upload to the correct target. Without it, Claude would have either guessed at the connector type or given a generic list of possibilities covering USB / serial / pogo pins — effectively useless for purchasing a specific cable.

### Example 7 — Unclear Output Preference After Complex Request

**Original input:**
> "We are in a situation where I have acquired a house that we cannot be kicked out of... I'm very careful with the types of jobs that I get... he's acting like I'm not trying at all... I'm trying to find a way to properly respond to him... that's what I was going to say but stopped to consult u"
*(draft text included)*

After receiving Claude's draft response, user asked:
> "Id rather address it before he does by adding 'you can say you're trying to help but by....... then you're only......' Address it all. Close the gaps"

**What was unclear:** "Address it all" after a dense emotional input could mean: (a) reference every complaint from the preamble, (b) preemptively answer counterarguments the roommate might make, or (c) both.

**Clarifying question Claude asked:**
> "Before I write anything I need to know one thing. What's the goal here? Option A... Option B... Option C..."

*(Same as Example 3 — confirmed as a recurring pattern type)*

## Reusable Template Library

Five templates covering the most common ambiguity types in ADHD-style input.

---

### Template 1: Typo / Phonetic Corruption

**When to apply:**
Input contains a word that produces no valid response when taken literally but makes sense if interpreted phonetically or as a voice-to-text error.

**Signal words:** unusual product names, brand names that don't match anything known, single-letter substitutions ("unison hat" → "Unisom SleepTabs"), internal contradiction between context and named term.

**Template:**

> "I think there might be a typo — are you asking about [reconstructed term]?"

**Variants:**
- "Did you mean [X]? The word [Y] isn't matching anything, but [X] fits the context."
- "Just confirming — when you wrote [garbled term], did you mean [reconstructed term]? I can answer the question once I know which one."

**What NOT to do:** Don't answer as if the corrupted word was intentional. Don't guess silently and answer the wrong thing. Don't ask for full re-statement — just confirm the one ambiguous term.

---

### Template 2: Goal / Intent Type Unclear

**When to apply:**
Input contains a real question but could be answered at several different levels or in several different modes: (a) exploration, (b) execution, (c) validation, (d) editing. The right answer for each mode is completely different.

**Signal words:** "how do I," "what should I do about," "help me with," plus a description of a situation rather than a specific deliverable.

**Template:**

> "Before I [write/answer/build], I need to know: what's the goal here?
>
> A: [mode 1 description]
> B: [mode 2 description]
> C: [mode 3 description if applicable]
>
> Which one fits?"

**Variants:**
- "Are you looking for [option 1] or [option 2]? Those are different conversations."
- "Do you want me to [X] or [Y]? Both are possible but the answer looks completely different depending."

**When to use the short form:** If only two options are live and the distinction is obvious, use: "Are you looking for X or Y?"

**What NOT to do:** Don't answer all possible modes simultaneously. Don't default to the most elaborate mode without asking. Don't ask more than three options — pick the two or three most likely and offer "something else" as a fallback.

---

### Template 3: Missing Referent ("It," "This," "That")

**When to apply:**
Input contains a pronoun ("it," "this," "that," "them") whose referent is either absent or ambiguous because it appeared in a prior conversation, a file, or an image not described in words.

**Signal words:** "what does it do," "how does this work," "why did that happen," "the one I sent you," plus no clear noun antecedent in the same message.

**Template:**

> "What specifically is the [it/this/that] you're asking about? I want to make sure I'm answering the right thing."

**Variants:**
- "When you say [pronoun], are you referring to [option A] or [option B]?"
- "Just to confirm — '[quoted phrase]' — which [thing] are you asking about?"

**For image-based referents:**
- "The photo shows [label/surface/packaging] but not the [port/connector/part] itself. Can you take a photo showing [specific target] directly?"

**What NOT to do:** Don't guess and answer the wrong referent. Don't ask the user to "be more specific" without naming what's missing. Don't acknowledge every possible interpretation in your question — name the two most likely and confirm.

---

### Template 4: Compound Request with Unclear Scope Boundary

**When to apply:**
Input contains multiple questions or deliverables embedded in a single message, and it's unclear whether all of them should be addressed together, sequentially, or whether some supersede others.

**Signal words:** Long messages with multiple topics, messages containing both emotional context and a practical request, "and also," "but also," nested sub-questions.

**Template:**

> "There are [X] things embedded in that message. Which one is the actual task right now?
>
> A: [first task]
> B: [second task]
> C: [third task if applicable]"

**Variants (for editing tasks specifically):**
- "Do you want me to [tighten/restructure/clean language/leave your voice intact and fix only errors]?"
- "What do you want kept exactly as-is, and what's okay to change?"

**Short-form variant for two-part compound:**
- "Is the real question [X], or is it [Y]?"

**What NOT to do:** Don't address all embedded tasks unless the user confirms they all apply. Don't answer the first task and ignore the rest. Don't ask about every individual piece — identify the two or three primary tasks and present them as a choice.

---

### Template 5: Context Required Before Answer Is Possible

**When to apply:**
The input contains a question that is technically clear but cannot be answered correctly without one specific piece of context that is missing — usually: (a) the device/product model or version, (b) the desired output format or destination, (c) the user's current situation or constraint.

**Signal words:** Troubleshooting questions, recommendation requests, "what should I use," "how do I fix," "which one is best for," without the situational context that determines the answer.

**Template:**

> "To answer that I need one piece of context first: [specific thing needed].
>
> [Optional: brief reason why that changes the answer]"

**Variants:**
- "The answer depends entirely on [X]. What is [X] in your situation?"
- "Before I can tell you what to buy/use/do: [single specific question]."
- "I can answer this but [X] will change the answer completely. What's [X]?"

**What NOT to do:** Don't give a generic list of all possible answers. Don't ask for more context than necessary — identify the one blocker and ask only for that. Don't explain at length why you can't answer yet; just ask the one question.

---

## When NOT to Ask a Clarifying Question

Use T18 (direct response bypass) when:
- The input is syntactically complete and the domain is unambiguous
- The only ambiguity is word order or phrasing, not meaning
- Answering both possible interpretations takes less time than asking
- The user is in an urgent or frustrated state (repeated follow-ups, profanity, "you already said this")

Asking a clarifying question when the answer is obvious from context creates friction. The templates above apply only when the question genuinely cannot be answered correctly without the missing information.

---

## Confidence Thresholds for Asking

| Situation | Action |
|-----------|--------|
| Core term is corrupted or missing | Always ask — answer is impossible without it |
| Goal type is ambiguous between 2+ modes | Ask if modes produce very different outputs; proceed if outputs are similar |
| Pronoun referent is ambiguous | Ask if more than 2 candidates; guess-and-check if only 2 and one is much more likely |
| Scope is unclear in edit/write task | Ask once; don't ask follow-up scope questions in the same session |
| Context is missing for recommendation | Ask for the one blocker; don't enumerate all the context that *might* matter |

---

## Integration with Pipeline

These templates activate during **Stage 1 (Translation)** when T01 (Extract-Core-Question) cannot resolve a referent, mode, or term with confidence ≥ 60%.

The output of a clarifying question is a **CLARIFICATION_NEEDED** flag that pauses Stage 2 (Routing) and Stage 3 (Composition) until the user's response resolves the ambiguity.

After the user responds, the pipeline resumes from Stage 1 with the resolved input.

The **6.14 confidence thresholds** apply:
- ≥ 80% confidence in translation → proceed automatically
- 60–79% → proceed with warning ("interpreted as: [X]")
- < 60% → trigger clarifying question from the appropriate template above
