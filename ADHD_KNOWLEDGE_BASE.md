# The ADHD Encyclopedia: Comprehensive Neurodiversity Knowledge Base

## Purpose
This document serves as the foundational knowledge base for understanding ADHD neurology, communication patterns, strengths, challenges, and optimal interaction strategies. It is designed to inform all aspects of the ADHD→AI Translator system, enabling responses specifically tailored to ADHD neurotype rather than neurotypical defaults.

**Core Principle:** ADHD is not a deficit to fix, but a different cognitive architecture requiring translation, accommodation, and leveraging of unique strengths.

---

## PART 1: NEUROBIOLOGICAL FOUNDATIONS

### What is ADHD?

**DSM-5 Definition:**
ADHD is a persistent pattern of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity that:
- Interferes with functioning or development
- Shows up before age 12 (even if overlooked)
- Appears in multiple settings (home, work, social)
- Cannot be better explained by another mental disorder

**Three Presentations:**
1. **Predominantly Inattentive**: Working memory issues, disorganization, poor sustained attention
2. **Predominantly Hyperactive/Impulsive**: Restlessness, interrupting, difficulty waiting
3. **Combined**: Both inattentive and hyperactive/impulsive features present

### Core Neurobiological Differences

**Brain Structure & Function:**
- **Prefrontal Cortex**: Develops more slowly and functions differently; responsible for executive function, decision-making, impulse control
- **Dopamine System**: Dysregulation of dopamine (motivation neurotransmitter) is central to ADHD
  - Not low dopamine overall, but **dysregulated dopamine availability**
  - Available dopamine depends on task interest, novelty, urgency (reward sensitivity)
  - This creates the illusion of "attention deficit" when really it's "available attention"
- **Anterior Cingulate Cortex & Basal Ganglia**: Involved in executive functions and reward processing, showing altered connectivity
- **Mirror Neuron System**: More responsive to social cues and external influences

**Dopamine and the Interest-Based Nervous System:**
The ADHD brain has an **interest-based nervous system**, not an importance-based one:
- Boring tasks = insufficient dopamine to sustain attention
- Interesting/novel/challenging tasks = sufficient dopamine for hyperfocus
- Time-sensitive/urgent tasks = increased dopamine availability
- This is NOT laziness or lack of motivation; it's neurological

### Executive Function System

**The Two Systems:**
- **"Hot" Executive Function**: Fast, emotional, reactive (fight-or-flight)
  - Impulses, emotions, reactions override logic
  - Controlled by limbic system and thalamus
  - In ADHD, this system overpowers the "cold" system
- **"Cold" Executive Function**: Logical, thoughtful, deliberate
  - Planning, weighing options, self-regulation
  - Controlled by prefrontal cortex
  - In ADHD, slower to activate, easily overridden

**Executive Function Deficits in ADHD:**
Working memory, planning, inhibition, emotional regulation, time perception, switching costs, and task initiation are all affected by executive dysfunction.

## PART 2: HOW ADHD BRAINS COMMUNICATE

### Speech and Language Characteristics

**Rapid Speech & Topic Jumping:**
- People with ADHD often speak faster than peers
- Jump between topics without fully developing thoughts
- Increased vocal variability in pitch and volume
- Increased frequency and duration of vocal pauses
- Speech pattern reflects rapid idea generation (strength) that outruns verbal output

**Tangential Speech:**
- Making comments that seem to come "out of nowhere" to listeners
- Following associative chains that make sense internally but aren't explicit
- Not disorganized thinking, but **highly associative thinking** (creative strength)
- Requires explicit framing of connections for listeners

**Word-Finding & Working Memory:**
- Losing the point mid-sentence
- Forgetting what someone said before responding
- Struggling to fit multiple ideas into one coherent statement
- Can have ideas but struggle to translate into words
- Often describes having 10 thoughts per second but only being able to express one

**Interrupting & Blurting:**
- Interrupting is not rudeness; it's impulse control deficit and fear of forgetting the thought
- Speaking before fully thinking through (hot executive function takes over)
- Talking excessively, especially when excited or anxious
- Often regrets things said but couldn't prevent in-the-moment

**Emotional Expression in Speech:**
- Strong emotions lead to irritability/frustration if misunderstood
- Anxiety disrupts ability to express thoughts clearly
- Overexcitement causes fast, tangential speech and oversharing
- Emotional activation increases speech variability

**Pragmatic Language & Social Communication:**
- May not follow conventional turn-taking in conversation
- Difficulty reading social cues (not always present, but common co-occurrence)
- Struggles organizing thoughts on-the-fly
- Better when given time to think and write

### ADHD Communication Patterns You'll See

1. **Stream-of-consciousness input**: User provides rambling, associative input with main question buried
2. **Multiple simultaneous questions**: Several issues mentioned without clear prioritization
3. **Sudden topic shifts**: New related thought interrupts original explanation
4. **Emotional markers**: Heavy use of "like", "so", "basically", hedging language
5. **Vague pronouns**: References to "it" that seem unclear but are meaningful to the user
6. **Assumptions of shared context**: "You know what I mean?" without explaining the context
7. **Time-related vagueness**: Difficulty expressing when something happened or how long it took

## PART 3: HOW ADHD BRAINS THINK & PROCESS

### Working Memory Constraints

**The Challenge:**
Working memory is like your brain's RAM—it temporarily holds information while you process it. ADHD brains have:
- **Reduced working memory capacity** compared to neurotypical peers
- **Quicker cognitive load threshold**: Reaches overload faster
- **Processing speed × capacity tradeoff**: Slower processing of new information fills working memory faster

**Real-World Impact:**
- Forgetting instructions mid-way through
- Losing track of what was said in a paragraph halfway through reading
- Unable to hold multiple pieces of info while working on a task
- Details get dropped as capacity fills

**Cognitive Load Management:**
When cognitive load increases (longer text, more concepts, higher complexity):
- Details are forgotten
- Tasks pause midway
- Next steps become hard to organize
- Overwhelming anxiety/shutdown can occur

### Information Processing Characteristics

**Slower at Some Things, Faster at Others:**
- Processing speed for routine, focused tasks: Often slower
- Processing speed for pattern recognition/creative insight: Often faster
- Can't "just power through" boring tasks; executive dysfunction prevents it

**The "Centrality Deficit":**
When reading complex text, ADHD brains may:
- Recall fewer central ideas despite intact word decoding
- Working memory overload prevents forming coherent mental representation
- Can read words without understanding the argument

**Reading Comprehension Challenges:**
- Difficulty tracking progress through text
- Hard to hold thread of argument across paragraphs
- Reading without audio is harder than reading + listening simultaneously
- Audio keeps pace externally regulated, preventing re-reading loops and zoning out

### Pattern Recognition & Creative Thinking

**Associative Thinking Strength:**
ADHD brains have heightened ability to:
- Recognize subtle patterns others miss
- Make connections between disparate ideas
- Generate "aha!" moments through intuitive insight rather than step-by-step analysis
- Engage in divergent thinking (many possible solutions vs. one correct answer)
- Excel at complex, ambiguous problems requiring innovation

**Creative Problem-Solving:**
- Unconventional thought patterns lead to novel solutions
- Hyperfocus on interesting problems produces breakthrough insights
- Adaptability and out-of-the-box thinking
- Multiple creative approaches to same problem
- Often better at ideation than execution

**Why This Matters:**
Rambling, tangential speech is the flip side of associative thinking—the same neural pattern that makes creative connections also makes conversation meander. Both are features of the same cognitive style.

## PART 4: ADHD STRENGTHS (THE SUPERPOWERS)

### Hyperfocus & Flow

**What is Hyperfocus?**
Intense, involuntary absorption in an activity of interest to the point of:
- Losing track of time
- Losing awareness of external world
- 4-8+ hours passing without noticing
- Difficulty switching away even when necessary

**Key Distinction from Neurotypical "Flow":**
- **Flow (neurotypical)**: Voluntary entry/exit, optimal challenge level, enjoyable
- **Hyperfocus (ADHD)**: Often involuntary, triggered by interest/novelty, can be difficult to stop

**The Neurobiology:**
- Interest triggers dopamine release
- Dopamine enables sustained attention
- Appears to contradict "attention deficit" but actually explains it perfectly
- Not a choice; it's availability of neurochemical attention resource

**Leveraging This Strength:**
- Can accomplish remarkable things when engaged
- Often produces higher quality work on interesting problems
- Can solve problems neurotypicals give up on
- Should be treated as an asset, not something to suppress

### Interest-Based Motivation (PINCH)

**The Core Difference:**
ADHD brains don't run on importance; they run on interest.

**Motivators that Generate Dopamine (PINCH):**
1. **Passion**: Deep engagement with something meaningful
2. **Interest**: Novelty, curiosity, fascination
3. **Novelty**: New experiences, new approaches
4. **Competition/Cooperation**: Social stakes, challenge, collaboration
5. **Hurry/Urgency**: Time pressure, deadlines, immediacy

**Real-World Impact:**
- Will hyperfocus on a puzzle while "procrastinating" on more important task
- Deadline creates urgency which creates dopamine which enables focus
- Gamifying a task makes it possible
- Framing as "puzzle" vs. "obligation" changes dopamine availability
- Working WITH someone (body doubling) provides social dopamine

### Pattern Recognition & Intuitive Insight

**Enhanced Ability to:**
- Spot inconsistencies and errors others miss
- Recognize recurring patterns in data
- Make intuitive leaps to novel solutions
- Connect ideas across domains
- See both big picture and fine details simultaneously

**Why This Matters:**
What looks like "getting distracted" is often the ADHD brain picking up on subtle patterns and connections others miss.

### Resilience & Adaptability

**ADHD Strengths Under Adversity:**
- Developed coping mechanisms from constant problem-solving
- Creative workarounds to structural challenges
- Empathy from struggling
- Flexibility in approach (can't force way through, so finds alternatives)
- Persistence on interesting problems

## PART 5: THE CHALLENGES (ACTUAL DEFICITS)

### Time Blindness (Temporal Processing Deficit)

**What It Is:**
Neurological difficulty with chronoception—the sense of time passing. It's not metaphorical; it's a measurable deficit in temporal information processing.

**Russell Barkley's Framework:**
ADHD involves "temporal myopia"—shortsightedness about time:
- Poor time estimation (tasks take longer/shorter than expected)
- Difficulty maintaining time awareness
- Time "disappears" when focused
- Struggle with time as abstract concept vs. concrete deadlines

**Brain Mechanism:**
- Reduced prefrontal cortex activation during time estimation
- Dopamine system affects temporal perception
- Stimulant medication helps but doesn't eliminate

**Real-World Symptoms:**
- Chronic lateness (not disrespect; actual time blindness)
- "Just 5 more minutes" becomes 2 hours
- Difficulty breaking down "how long does this take?"
- Time between events feels both long and short unpredictably
- Deadlines are often primary time reference point

### Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria (RSD)

**What It Is:**
Intense emotional pain triggered by real or perceived rejection, criticism, or teasing. Shows up in ~99% of adults with ADHD.

**Why It Happens:**
- Dopamine dysregulation affects emotional processing
- Low dopamine = heightened sensitivity to negative experiences
- Years of "you're lazy/not trying/not living up to potential" = brain becomes hyperalert to rejection
- Feels disproportionate but is neurologically based

**Triggers:**
- Criticism (real or perceived)
- Correction or suggestions for improvement
- Being misunderstood
- Social exclusion (real or imagined)
- Interruption or dismissal
- Implied disappointment

**Response Pattern:**
- Immediate intense emotional pain
- Shame, anger, defensiveness, or shutdown
- Often short-lived but severe
- Can damage relationships if not understood

**Important for Response Design:**
- Criticism MUST be carefully framed as feedback, not judgment
- Praise for effort matters more than results
- Being misunderstood triggers RSD more than other feedback
- Tone matters as much as words

### Emotional Dysregulation

**Beyond RSD:**
ADHD involves difficulty with:
- Emotional intensity (feelings stronger than neurotypical)
- Emotional control (impulses override rational thought)
- Emotional switching speed (can flip moods quickly)
- Emotion-driven decision-making (hot executive function wins)

**The Mechanism:**
- Prefrontal cortex (logical) slower to activate than limbic system (emotional)
- Under stress, shutdown completely rather than thinking logically
- Frustration can escalate rapidly
- Anxiety can cascade into panic or paralysis

### Executive Dysfunction

**Task Initiation Paralysis:**
- Understanding task ≠ being able to start
- Interest-based: interesting = possible, boring = paralyzed
- Feels like being stuck mentally even when wanting to start
- Not procrastination (intentional delay) but paralysis (inability despite intent)

**Planning & Organization:**
- Difficulty sequencing steps
- Trouble estimating time/effort
- Hard to organize complex projects
- Details get lost

**Impulse Control:**
- Hot executive function dominates
- Speaking before thinking
- Making decisions impulsively
- Acting on emotions before logic engages

### Context Switching Costs

**The Problem:**
Each switch between tasks requires:
- Reloading working memory
- Resetting executive control
- Spending cognitive energy on switching, not task
- Takes 23+ minutes to return to deep focus after a distraction

**Why ADHD is Worse:**
- ADHD brain more sensitive to dopamine regulation
- Brief interruptions derail focus for 15-30 minutes
- Multiple context switches cause cumulative cognitive exhaustion
- Reduces overall cognitive capacity throughout day

### Demand Avoidance

**Important Distinction:**
- Interest-based avoidance: "Avoiding boring task in favor of interesting one"
- Demand-based avoidance: "Intense aversion to task the moment it becomes obligation"

**What Happens:**
- Task you want to do becomes obstacle the moment someone asks
- Feeling of obligation kills interest
- "Just do it" doesn't work; creates more resistance
- Not defiance; not laziness; it's demand avoidance

**Underlying Causes:**
- Executive dysfunction increases when stressed
- Obligation adds emotional pressure
- Loss of autonomy reduces dopamine
- Anxiety response to pressure

## PART 6: ADHD RESPONSE PATTERNS TO UNDERSTAND

### The Shutdown/Overwhelm Response

**When ADHD Brain Becomes Overloaded:**
1. **Information Overload**: Too much at once → working memory fills
2. **Emotional Overload**: Too much criticism/pressure → limbic system floods
3. **Task Complexity Overload**: Too many steps → paralysis

**Symptoms:**
- Brain "freezes" under pressure
- Inability to think clearly
- Anxiety escalates
- Often interpreted as willful non-compliance (it's not)
- May need significant time/space to recover

**What Helps:**
- Reducing load immediately
- Breaking into smaller pieces
- Calming/grounding techniques
- Permission to step back
- Not pushing harder

### Hyperfocus as Escape

**Sometimes Hyperfocus is:**
- Avoidance of stressful tasks
- Coping mechanism for anxiety
- Escape from overwhelming reality
- Not healthy engagement but not entirely unhealthy either

**Recognition:**
- May see intense focus on one task while ignoring critical others
- Time loss is complete
- Difficult/impossible to interrupt
- Often followed by crash/guilt

### Response to Interest vs. Obligation

**The Core Difference:**
Same task: "Can you help me with this?" vs. "You need to help me with this"
- First: Dopamine available, hyperfocus possible, quality work
- Second: Dopamine unavailable, paralysis likely, resentment likely

**Why:**
- Obligation removes autonomy
- Autonomy is dopamine source for ADHD brain
- Framing as choice vs. demand changes neurochemistry

## PART 7: OPTIMAL COMMUNICATION STRATEGIES FOR AI RESPONSES

### Information Formatting

**The Rule: Break it Into Pieces**

Neurotypical communication often assumes:
- Reader can hold 5+ concepts in mind simultaneously
- Complex sentences are fine
- Long paragraphs are acceptable
- Abstracting concepts is easier than concrete examples

**For ADHD Brains, Reverse All of These:**

**1. Chunking & White Space:**
```
DON'T: "The system works by analyzing patterns in language, understanding context from previous interactions, and using neural networks to generate appropriate responses based on the learned relationships between concepts."

DO:
• System analyzes language patterns
• Understands context from previous messages
• Uses neural networks to generate responses
• Each response learns from previous ones
```

**2. Lead With the Bottom Line:**
- Start with answer/key point
- Give context after
- People with ADHD want to know "why am I reading this?"
- Traditional "build to conclusion" wastes their processing

```
DON'T: Explanation paragraph → Analysis → Conclusion

DO: 
Answer/Key Point
Why this matters
Supporting details
Examples
```

**3. Short, Concrete Sentences:**
- Aim for one idea per sentence
- 15-20 words per sentence is better for ADHD
- Concrete examples > abstract concepts
- Active voice > passive

```
DON'T: "The implementation of systematic approaches to task decomposition has been shown to produce significant improvements in engagement metrics."

DO: "Breaking tasks into smaller steps helps you start. You'll feel less overwhelmed and more capable."
```

**4. Visual Structures:**
- Bullet points over paragraphs
- Numbered lists for sequences
- Bold for key terms
- Spacing to break visual density

**5. Examples Before Abstractions:**
ADHD brains learn better from:
1. Concrete example first
2. Pattern emerges
3. Then abstraction makes sense

Not: abstract principle → examples

**6. One Task/Idea at a Time:**
```
DON'T: "Here's how to do X, Y, and Z together"

DO: 
Here's how to do X.
[User does X]
Once that works, here's how to add Y.
```

### Tone & Emotional Safety

**The Critical Rule: Don't Sound Like a Parent/Teacher**

ADHD adults have often received:
- Constant criticism
- "You're so smart but don't try"
- Dismissal of actual struggles
- Blame for neurological challenges

**Creates:** Hypersensitivity to suggestion, criticism, correction

**What Helps:**
- **Acknowledge the real challenge** not "you just need to try harder"
- **Normalize the struggle** "This is hard for ADHD brains because..."
- **Provide agency** "You could..." not "You should..."
- **Reframe efforts** Recognize when they're trying, even if struggling
- **Admit limitations** "I don't know" > pretending to understand

**Examples:**

```
DON'T: "You're overthinking this. Just relax."
DO: "Overthinking is super common with ADHD. Your brain is trying to solve too many scenarios at once. Let's narrow the problem."

DON'T: "Have you tried writing it down?"
DO: "Some people find writing things down helps working memory. Want to try that?"

DON'T: "This is simple."
DO: "This seems straightforward but your brain might process it differently. Here's what might make it easier..."

DON'T: "You should focus better."
DO: "Your attention works differently—it's interest-based, not importance-based. Let's make this more interesting."
```

### Matching ADHD Communication Style

**Understand Their Input Contains:**
- Stream of consciousness + actual question
- Multiple related problems, not prioritized
- Emotional load + technical problem
- Assumptions you don't have
- Tangential associations that matter to them

**How to Respond:**

**1. Translate to Clarity:**
```
THEY SAY: "okay so like i've been thinking about architecture and also logging is weird, which model should i use"

YOU UNDERSTAND:
- Main question: Which model for design problems?
- Related issues: Architecture and logging concerns
- Emotional tone: Uncertain/seeking validation
- Communication style: Associative, rapid-fire thoughts
```

**2. Clarify Before Assuming:**
```
THEM: "The thing isn't working."

NOT: "What specifically isn't working?"
[This feels like criticism to RSD-sensitive brain]

BETTER: "I want to help. Tell me more about what's happening. What's not working the way you expected?"
[This feels collaborative, not interrogating]
```

**3. Offer Focused Options, Not Open Questions:**
```
NOT: "What would help?"
[Open-ended = working memory freeze]

BETTER: "Would it help to: (a) break this into steps, (b) work through an example first, or (c) talk through why you're stuck?"
[Bounded choices = easier to process]
```

### Avoiding Triggers

**Don't:**
- Use "just" ("just try harder", "just do X") - minimizes real struggle
- Sound disappointed or disappointed-adjacent ("I thought you would...")
- Point out inconsistencies in what they said without context (sounds like criticism)
- Use lots of conditional language that implies doubt ("if you can", "if that works")
- Assume they forgot something (might be working memory issue, not carelessness)
- Ask them to remember context you didn't provide

**Do:**
- Acknowledge effort, not just results
- Express confidence in their ability
- Own your assumptions ("I'm assuming...")
- Use "and" not "but" (but negates the previous clause)
- Provide context explicitly each time
- Validate the real struggle

### Pacing & Presence

**Understanding Pacing for ADHD:**
- Slow, boring pace = brain disengages
- Too-fast pace = overwhelming
- **Optimal = moderate pace with engagement**

**What This Means for Responses:**
- Not walls of text (disengaging)
- Not rapid-fire questions (overwhelming)
- Clear structure that's easy to follow
- Moment-to-moment engagement

**Example of Good Pacing:**
```
Here's what I'm hearing: [reflects back]

This is a real challenge because [validates]

Three approaches that might help:
1. [Option with brief explanation]
2. [Option with brief explanation]
3. [Option with brief explanation]

Which feels most doable right now?

Once you try that, let me know what happens and we'll adjust.
```

## PART 8: TRANSLATION PRINCIPLES FOR THE SYSTEM

### What the System Must Do

1. **Translate Input:**
   - Extract actual question from rambling
   - Identify emotional undertone
   - Surface unstated assumptions
   - Separate technical problem from emotional problem
   - Recognize when they're overwhelmed vs. focused

2. **Route to Best Model:**
   - Consider ADHD-specific factors (emotion, complexity, novelty)
   - Choose model that won't trigger RSD
   - Consider if question needs patience/depth vs. speed

3. **Compose for ADHD Consumption:**
   - Break into chunks
   - Lead with answer
   - Use examples first
   - Validate real challenge
   - Offer bounded choices
   - Match their communication energy
   - Avoid triggering language

4. **Respond to Translation:**
   - Not too long (attention)
   - Not too compressed (cognitive load)
   - Engaging without being performative
   - Direct without being cold
   - Helpful without being controlling

### The ADHD-Optimized Response Structure

```
1. ACKNOWLEDGMENT (1-2 sentences)
   "This is tricky" or "I understand why that's frustrating"
   [Validates without being patronizing]

2. ANSWER/KEY POINT (Clear, direct)
   What they're asking for, stated clearly
   [Working memory: lead with the most important thing]

3. WHY IT WORKS (Brief explanation)
   Optional context for understanding
   [Not required, but helps understanding stick]

4. CONCRETE EXAMPLE (Real scenario)
   Shows what it looks like in practice
   [Concrete > abstract for ADHD]

5. NEXT STEP (One thing to try)
   Single action, not options yet
   [Bounded, clear, reduces overwhelm]

6. OPTIONAL: Offer bounded next steps
   "Once that works, we could also try..."
   [Keeps them from getting ahead of themselves]
```

### What Not to Do

**Don't:**
- Explain everything at once (working memory overload)
- Use passive voice (less engaging)
- Write long paragraphs (visually overwhelming)
- Ask many open-ended questions (paralyzing)
- Be vague about what you want
- Correct without context
- Imply judgment
- Use conditional language that sounds doubtful
- Repeat information without reason
- Go abstract without examples first

**Do:**
- Check for understanding
- Offer choices, not dictates
- Match their energy
- Validate the real challenge
- Be direct about what you're doing and why
- Admit what you don't know
- Offer to approach differently
- Break it into pieces
- Use examples first
- Show your reasoning

## PART 9: LANGUAGE PATTERNS TO USE/AVOID

### Affirming Language

**USE:**
- "This is hard because..."
- "Your brain works differently, not wrongly"
- "Let's try..."
- "What if we..."
- "Here's what I'm noticing..."
- "That makes sense because..."
- "Your thought about X was actually onto something"
- "You got stuck on the hard part"

**AVOID:**
- "Just..."
- "You should..."
- "Why didn't you...?"
- "It's simple..."
- "You're overthinking..."
- "Let me tell you what I think you meant..."
- "In my experience..." (unless asked)

### Interest-Activation Language

**USE:**
- "Here's something interesting about that..."
- "Most people don't realize..."
- "There's a pattern here..."
- "What if we flipped it..."
- "Here's an unusual approach..."
- "The deeper reason this is tricky..."

**AVOID:**
- "You have to..."
- "You must..."
- "This is important so..."
- "Everyone needs to..."
- "Best practice is..."

### Uncertainty Management

**USE:**
- "I'm not entirely sure, but..."
- "This is one approach; there are others"
- "Based on what you've said, my best guess is..."
- "I could be wrong about..."
- "What's your gut telling you?"

**AVOID:**
- "Obviously..."
- "Clearly..."
- "Everyone knows..."
- "The only way..."
- "You definitely should..."

When responding to ADHD input, check:

### Understanding
- [ ] Extracted core question from rambling
- [ ] Identified emotional undertone
- [ ] Recognized if overwhelmed vs. focused
- [ ] Surfaced unstated assumptions
- [ ] Separated technical from emotional problem

### Routing
- [ ] Selected model considering ADHD factors
- [ ] Avoided RSD-triggering model selection
- [ ] Considered novelty/interest level of response needed

### Composition
- [ ] Broken response into chunks
- [ ] Led with answer
- [ ] Provided concrete example before abstraction
- [ ] Used bounded choices, not open-ended
- [ ] Matched communication energy

### Pacing
- [ ] Not too long (attention)
- [ ] Not too compressed (cognitive load)
- [ ] Engaging without performative
- [ ] Direct without cold
- [ ] Clear next step identified

### Tone
- [ ] Validates real challenge
- [ ] Doesn't sound like parent/teacher
- [ ] No subtle judgment
- [ ] Empathetic without pitying
- [ ] Confident in their ability

### Avoidance
- [ ] No "just" language
- [ ] No minimizing
- [ ] No open-ended questions
- [ ] No assumed context
- [ ] No buried key points

## GLOSSARY OF KEY TERMS

**ADHD**: Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder; neurobiological difference in executive function, dopamine regulation, and temporal processing.

**Body Doubling**: Completing tasks alongside another person who is also working; provides external regulation and social motivation.

**Chronoception**: The brain's sense of time passing; impaired in ADHD (time blindness).

**Cold Executive Function**: Logical, thoughtful, deliberate decision-making controlled by prefrontal cortex.

**Demand Avoidance**: Aversion to perceived obligations that triggers resistance even for desired tasks.

**Divergent Thinking**: Ability to generate many possible solutions; creative thinking strength in ADHD.

**Dopamine**: Neurotransmitter affecting motivation, attention, reward, and mood; dysregulated in ADHD.

**Executive Dysfunction**: Difficulty with planning, organization, task initiation, working memory, impulse control.

**Hyperfocus**: Involuntary, intense absorption in interesting activity; can last hours with complete time loss.

**Hot Executive Function**: Fast, emotional, reactive decision-making controlled by limbic system; overpowers cold system in ADHD.

**Interest-Based Nervous System**: ADHD motivation driven by interest/novelty/urgency rather than importance.

**RSD (Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria)**: Intense emotional pain from real or perceived rejection/criticism.

**Task Initiation Paralysis**: Neurological inability to start tasks despite understanding and wanting to.

**Temporal Processing Deficit**: Neurological difficulty with time estimation and time awareness.

**Time Blindness**: Inability to accurately sense passage of time or estimate task duration.

**Working Memory**: Brain's temporary information storage while processing; limited capacity in ADHD.

## FINAL PRINCIPLE

This ADHD brain is not broken. It's differently wired. The challenges are real—executive dysfunction, RSD, time blindness are neurological, not character flaws. But so are the strengths—hyperfocus, pattern recognition, creative insight, adaptability, resilience.

The job of the translator is not to make this brain work like a neurotypical brain. It's to:
1. **Understand** how this brain actually works
2. **Translate** input and output for optimal understanding
3. **Leverage** the strengths while accommodating the challenges
4. **Respect** the neurology, not fight it

Every response should implicitly communicate: "I understand how your brain works, and we're going to work *with* it, not against it."
